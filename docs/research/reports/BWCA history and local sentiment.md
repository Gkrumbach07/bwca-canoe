# Every fight left stones in the woods

The Boundary Waters and Quetico are not untouched country. They are country that was used hard — Ojibwe homeland, fur-trade highway, pine and pulp cut, resort coast, mining frontier — and then protected in stages over about seventy years. Every stage was fought, and every stage cost some local people their livelihood or their home. The pattern repeats: Ojibwe lands ceded in 1854 and 1866 for copper, pine and iron; logging rail lines pulled up by 1918–1930; fly-in resorts grounded by the 1949 airban and bought out from 1948 to about 1970; Ely's last iron mine shut in 1967; motors, snowmobiles and logging cut back by the 1978 Act; and a copper-nickel fight, begun with federal leases in 1966, that has swung back and forth with nearly every administration since 2016. As of November 2026 nothing has been mined. Local feeling has never been simply "Ely against the Cities." Pro-wilderness outfitters were Ely-born children of immigrant miners and were picketed by neighbours. Some resort families mourned their loss and later said they were glad of the result. Today retired miners speak against the copper mine and the son of a Bois Forte member speaks for it. What survives in the woods is small and physical: stone foundations, dock cribs, cairns, rail grades, pried-out ore pits and drill cores shelved in Hibbing. For a story about a dead guide's notebooks and a 1977 disappearance, this history supplies a real calendar, real kinds of people and real wounds. The story has to invent its specific crime, its missing person and any border-crossing plot, because the record does not contain them.

*Method note:* the underlying notes were gathered with curl from Wikipedia, MNopedia, the 1854 Treaty Authority, the NPS and USFS (some pages via the Wayback Machine), Quetico Superior *Wilderness News*, *Minnesota History*, MPR, MinnPost, the Pioneer Press, the Timberjay, the Cook County News-Herald, business histories and bwca.com and QuietJourney forum threads. Forum material is first-person and unverified, and is flagged as such. Book-length sources (Searle's *Saving Quetico-Superior*, Proescholdt, Rapson and Heinselman's *Troubled Waters*, Griffith's *Gunflint Falling*) were not read directly.

## Treaties traded Ojibwe land for pine and ore but kept the right to hunt it

People have lived in the region since roughly 8000 BC. The Forest Service lists Dakota, Cree, Assiniboine and Ojibwe here at contact. Wikipedia's line that the area was "sparsely populated by the Sioux" before the Ojibwe arrived is a simplification ([USFS heritage, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856); [Wikipedia BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness)). Grand Portage, *Gichi Onigaming*, the "Great Carrying Place," has been in use for perhaps 2,000 years ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/grpo/learn/historyculture/index.htm)). The land was managed, not wild. Red pine fire scars record burns every 10–20 years from the 1600s to 1905, which the Forest Service links to Border Lakes Ojibwe burning for blueberries, medicine plants and moose habitat. Then came about nine decades of fire suppression ([USFS, 2026](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r09/superior/newsroom/stories/long-history-fire)). Photographs show an Ojibwe village on Basswood Lake near Washington Island as late as 1903, with families building birchbark canoes ([USFS, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856)).

In the **Treaty of La Pointe (30 September 1854)** the Bois Forte, Grand Portage and Fond du Lac bands ceded the Arrowhead. The United States wanted copper, minerals and pine, and $90,000 of the payment went to settle Ojibwe debts to traders ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/treaty-la-pointe-1854)). The bands insisted on two things. Article 11 says those living in the ceded land "shall have the right to hunt and fish therein, until otherwise ordered by the President," and the treaty promised they would not be required to remove ([Kappler text, OSU](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-1854-0648)). The memory behind that demand was the **1850 Sandy Lake Tragedy**, when hundreds died walking home in winter after a late, meagre annuity payment ([1854 Treaty Authority booklet](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/images/ToHuntandFish.updated2020.pdf)). In **1866**, right after the brief Vermilion gold rush of 1865, the Bois Forte gave up their Lake Vermilion reserve in exchange for Nett Lake, with a school, a blacksmith shop and an $11,000 annuity ([Kappler 1866](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-bois-fort-band-1866-0916); [Wikipedia gold rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermilion_Lake_gold_rush)). The prospectors went home with reports of iron, and within twenty years Tower and Soudan stood on the ceded ground. That sequence — gold rumour, cession, iron towns — is the first instance of the region's recurring pattern: a mineral finding decides who keeps the land.

For most of the twentieth century Minnesota ignored the hunting right. The Treaty Authority calls this an "illegal extension of state jurisdiction" that coincided with allotment, boarding schools and state tourism interest ([1854TA booklet](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/images/ToHuntandFish.updated2020.pdf)). Ojibwe men worked as loggers and resort guides, and Ojibwe women sold wild rice, berries and maple syrup to tourists ([USFS, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856)). Gunflint Lodge credits its Ojibwe neighbours with guiding, cleaning cabins, waiting tables and minding the owner's children ([Gunflint Lodge](https://gunflint.com/history-of-gunflint/)). A Grand Portage member was cited for a moose in 1984. The band sued in 1985, and a 1988 agreement with the state reaffirmed the rights and created what is now the 1854 Treaty Authority. Band members exercising treaty rights need no BWCAW permit, only an 1854 ID ([1854TA](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/about/who-we-are); [1854TA FAQ](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/1854-faq-s)). So a 1977 story falls inside the decades when an Ojibwe hunter off-reservation was routinely treated as a poacher.

On the Canadian side, creating Quetico took the Sturgeon Lake reserve (IR 24C) from the Lac La Croix people, who renamed themselves Gakijiwanong Anishinaabe Nation in 2023. **The date is disputed.** The Quetico article says Ontario cancelled the reserve in 1915; the band's article ties the loss to the 1950 Provincial Parks Act ([Wikipedia Quetico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetico_Provincial_Park); [Wikipedia Gakijiwanong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac_La_Croix_First_Nation)). Minister Bud Wildman apologised formally in the legislature in 1991. A 1994 Agreement of Co-existence followed, under which Lac La Croix guides may run motors of 10 hp or less on six Quetico lakes, although the park otherwise banned motors in 1979 ([Wilderness News 2026](https://queticosuperior.org/a-snapshot-of-quetico-provincial-parks-past/)). Even Ernest Oberholtzer, the founding preservationist, later felt the 1940s fight had overlooked the Native population ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/ernest-c-oberholtzer-lifetime-advocate/)). Conservation here was paid for partly with Indigenous land, and an honest story should acknowledge that.

## Voyageurs, loggers and miners built the towns that would later resent the wilderness

From the 1730s to 1803, Grand Portage was the summer rendezvous of the Montreal fur trade. The North West Company kept its depot and Great Hall there and Fort Charlotte at the far end of the 8.5-mile carry ([Wikipedia GPNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument)). The Nor'Westers' route — Pigeon River, Rose Lake, Height of Land, Gunflint, Saganaga, Knife, Basswood, Crooked, Lac La Croix — is the game's border chain. The work was brutal. Men carried two 90-lb pièces per trip, kept 14-hour days at about 55 strokes a minute, and suffered hernias and drowning ([Wikipedia Voyageurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyageurs)). At Height of Land Portage a new man was initiated with a cedar bough dipped in the lake, a mock oath and "high wine." **Sources disagree on the portage's length:** 80 rods (~400 m) in one Wikipedia article, 700 yards in another ([Wikipedia Height of Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_of_Land_Portage)). The NWC left for Fort William in 1802–03. The 1842 Webster–Ashburton Treaty fixed the border on this route and kept "all the usual portages" free and open to both countries, which is why trails still cross the line ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/how-is-the-international-border-marked-in-the-bwca/)).

Big-pine logging arrived in the 1890s, after Michigan and Wisconsin had been cut over, and switched to pulp after 1929 ([USFS, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)). Many homestead claims of 1869–1900 were fraudulent fronts for timber companies (same source). Swallow & Hopkins ferried locomotives across lakes on scows ([USFS, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049821)) and is said to have hauled 350 million board feet over the Four Mile Portage in a decade. That railway was dismantled in 1918 ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/magical-history-tour-through-the-bwca/); [Basswood Beach](https://queticosuperior.org/basswood-beach-resort-canoe-trips/)). On the Gunflint side, the General Logging Co. pushed rail to Rose Lake until the 1929 Brule Lake fire and the Depression stopped it ([Wikipedia Rose Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_Lake_(Pigeon_River))). The Bull-of-the-Woods steam scow (c. 1893), the only known survivor of its kind, lies wrecked in Burntside Lake and is on the National Register ([Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)). Logging in the Pagami Creek country went on into the 1970s ([Wilderness News 2012](https://queticosuperior.org/1999-blowdown-forest-management-practices-impacts-pagami-creek-fire/)).

Iron made the towns. Tower and Soudan (from 1882–84) were Minnesota's first iron towns. Soudan went underground and ran under U.S. Steel's Oliver Iron Mining until 1962 ([Wikipedia Soudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soudan_Underground_Mine_State_Park)). Ely began as "Florence" in 1888. Its Pioneer Mine, opened in 1889, produced about 40% of the Vermilion Range's ore ([Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)). The workforce was Finnish, Slovene, Italian, Cornish and Scandinavian. The 1907 and 1916 strikes were mainly on the Mesabi, but Finnish blacklisting after 1907 cut Finns from 18% to 8% of miners. In 1916, rents on the Range ran 20% above Twin Cities levels and food 50–100% higher ([Wikipedia 1907 strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1907_Mesabi_Range_strike); [Wikipedia 1916 strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1916_Mesabi_Range_strike)). **The Pioneer closed on 1 April 1967.** It closed because steelmaking had changed, not because the ore ran out ([Timberjay 2007](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/forty-years-ago-the-pioneer-mine-shut-down,3199)). Many residents remember it as the day the town died, and some still say canoeists will never save Ely ([Timberjay 2017](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/fifty-years-gone-but-not-forgotten,13567)). Babbitt, by contrast, was a postwar taconite company town, founded from 1944 by Armco and Republic Steel's Reserve Mining ([Wikipedia Babbitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babbitt,_Minnesota)). Ely's population fell from 6,156 in 1930 to 4,820 in 1980 and 3,268 in 2020, with a median age of 47 ([Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)). That long decline sits under every later argument about the wilderness.

## Dams, planes and buyouts set the template of local loss, 1909–1964

Protection came in layers. **Superior National Forest was proclaimed on 13 February 1909, and Ontario's Quetico reserve on 1 April 1909**; Quetico became a provincial park in 1913. **The figures for the forest's original size conflict:** about 1,018,638 acres per *Wilderness News*, 644,114 per the USFS ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/quetico-superior-region-100-years-stewardship-beyond-boundaries/); [USFS, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)). Arthur Carhart's 1919–22 surveys led to a 640,000-acre roadless area in 1926. A 1952 court dated that area to "1927 or thereabouts," **a minor conflict** ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/old-photos-provide-peek-history/); [US v. Perko](https://static.case.law/f-supp/108/cases/0315-01.json)). The Forest Service ranger's name is spelled both Soderbeck and Soderback.

The first great fight was over water. In 1925 the paper magnate Edward Backus proposed seven border dams. Oberholtzer and a group of Minneapolis men who first met in secret formed the Quetico-Superior Council to stop him ([MNopedia Oberholtzer](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/oberholtzer-ernest-1884-1977)). *Wilderness News* says the dams would have raised water 80 feet and drowned Lac La Croix's islands. **That figure is unverified and likely exaggerated** ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/ernest-c-oberholtzer-lifetime-advocate/)). The **Shipstead-Newton-Nolan Act (10 July 1930)** barred homesteading and changes to water levels, and banned logging within 400 feet of shore ([US v. Perko](https://static.case.law/f-supp/108/cases/0315-01.json)).

The second fight was over aircraft, and it set the template for local grievance. By the early 1940s more than 40 resorts stood inside today's BWCAW, employing over 300 people. Ely ran up to 38 seaplane flights a day and was the largest freshwater seaplane base in the continental US ([Wilderness News, lodges I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/); [Timberjay 2012](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)). Resort counts for Basswood vary by date: 16 by the mid-1940s, more than 20 by the late 1950s ([Wilderness News, Rom](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/); [lodges II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)). The Thye-Blatnik Act (1948) put $500,000 toward buyouts, with in-lieu tax payments to the counties, and was renewed in 1956 and 1961 ([Wikipedia Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)). Truman's **Executive Order 10092 (17 December 1949)** closed the airspace below 4,000 ft over the roadless areas from 1 January 1951, with exemptions for rescue, emergencies and official business. **The grace-period date conflicts:** the Truman Library's transcription reads "1942," which is impossible for a 1949 order; the Eighth Circuit opinion gives 1 January 1952 ([Truman Library EO 10092](https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10092/executive-order-10092); [Perko v. US, 8th Cir. 1953](https://static.case.law/f2d/204/cases/0446-01.json)). Resort owners Perko, Skala and Zupancich and Ely pilot West defied the order and lost in 1952 and again on appeal in 1953. Judge Nordbye wrote that it had been canoe country since the days of the voyageurs ([US v. Perko](https://static.case.law/f-supp/108/cases/0315-01.json)). In 1952 two pilots still landing were caught and their planes confiscated ([Wikipedia Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)).

Both sides of that fight deserve a fair hearing. To the resort families, the pilots and much of Ely, the ban was a federal order that destroyed working businesses. Bill Rom hosted airban meetings, and someone set off an explosive near his house as a warning; *Wilderness News* notes that his view was not the popular one ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/)). To canoeists, an area reached by plane was no longer canoe country. Benny Ambrose, the Ottertrack guide, testified for the ban even though it cost him business ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/magical-history-tour-through-the-bwca/)). The fate of the buildings is the most usable detail. Former residents recall hauling cabins onto the ice and burning them on winter nights. By the late 1960s more than 40 resorts and over 90 private homes had been bought. Basswood Lodge's main building was bought in 1964, sold and hauled over the ice to become Snowbank Lodge, bought again after 1978, and finally sold to a Wisconsin county for $1 in 1985 ([lodges II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)). Feelings among those families did not settle into one view. Helen Koski's father's Crooked Lake lodge was ruined by the ban, yet she later said she was glad the lake did not fill with cabins ([Pioneer Press 2014](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)). A former Basswood guide in his eighties said he still thought about the work every day, and that canoeists spend far less in town than resort fishermen did ([Timberjay 2012](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)).

**"Operation Snowbird" does not appear in any source found.** Wikipedia, *Wilderness News* and the USFS history pages say nothing of a BWCA operation by that name; the name turns up only for military operations. The documented facts are smaller: cabins burned on the ice (undated), government purchase and removal, and confiscated planes.

## Two Wilderness Acts split Ely neighbour from neighbour, 1964–1978

The **1964 Wilderness Act** made the BWCA an "instant wilderness." It was the only area named in the Act with special exceptions, and it left logging and motors to the Secretary of Agriculture ([MinnPost 2015](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/); [Wikipedia BWCA Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)). MinnPost describes "eight years and 66 rewrites"; the Pioneer Press says 19 hearings and dozens of rewrites. The Selke Committee (1964) included a lumber executive. At a closed session, Forest Service scientist Bud Heinselman argued that at least 400,000 acres of virgin forest survived; his agency then barred him from any public role on the issue, even on his own time ([Proescholdt, *Minnesota History* 2014](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/64/v64i02p070-084.pdf)). **The Freeman Directives are dated January 1965 by Proescholdt and 15 December 1965 by Wikipedia.** The December date is probably the final regulations. In 1964 the Forest Service ruled snowmobiles "winter motorboats," and winter traffic past Ambrose's cabin soon outran the summer canoes ([*Minnesota History* 1994](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/54/v54i03p124-137.pdf)). Then came lawsuits. Judge Miles Lord stopped logging in virgin forest in 1973 and 1975; the Eighth Circuit reversed him in August 1976 ([Wikipedia BWCA Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)). The result left both sides feeling cheated: locals felt that what 1964 had promised was being taken away, and advocates felt the "wilderness" was not one.

The congressional fight followed. Rep. Jim Oberstar, whose district included Ely, proposed splitting the area into a smaller wilderness and a recreation area open to motors and logging. **The sources disagree on his bill:** Wikipedia gives H.R. 10247, 24 October 1975, 625,000 + 527,000 acres; MinnPost gives 1976, 600,000 + nearly 400,000 acres. Rep. Don Fraser of Minneapolis introduced a full-wilderness bill. **Its number also conflicts** — H.R. 14576 in Wikipedia, H.R. 2820 in Heinselman's 1977 testimony, probably a reintroduction in the new Congress ([Wikipedia BWCA Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act); [Wikipedia Heinselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Lee_Heinselman); [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)). Bud and Fran Heinselman founded Friends of the Boundary Waters Wilderness in May 1976. They lived in Washington at their own expense, and he carried tubes of maps through the Capitol ([Friends of the BW, 2020](https://www.friends-bwca.org/blog/bud-heinselman/)).

Ely boiled. Sports Illustrated, as quoted by MinnPost, reported bumper stickers reading "SIERRA CLUB KISS MY AXE," a burned Forest Service building, slashed tyres, and a pro-Fraser outfitter whose window was broken more than once ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)). In 1975 snowmobile-ban protesters barricaded Ely's roads on Fishing Opener and Memorial Day and picketed Bill Rom's Canoe Country Outfitters, the Ely-born "Canoe King," with signs calling for him to be run out of town. He sold the business that year ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/)). His daughter Becky said he was picketed by people he had lived with all his life ([Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)). A future lodge owner who was 17 in 1978 remembers car blockades turning canoeists away and a summer spent afraid. He also describes how locals felt: a tiny group set against a great national movement (same source).

**The set piece for a 1977 story is the Ely field hearing of 8 July 1977**, the day after a St. Paul hearing at which Rep. Willard Munger argued that national interests must outweigh local ones ([MPR 2017](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/01/23/how-ely-has-handled-economic-environmental-concerns)). More than 1,000 people packed the auditorium. Logging trucks lined the streets, and a dummy hung by the neck from a truck's boom with the names of Sigurd Olson and Heinselman pinned to it. When Rep. Bruce Vento called Olson, then 78, the hall booed for minutes, and Vento asked an aide about a way out if the crowd turned. Once it quieted, Olson argued that some places should be kept from development because people need them for solace and perspective ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/); [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)). **The accounts differ slightly on where the effigy hung** — on a logging truck (MinnPost, MPR) or outside the auditorium (Wikipedia's Heinselman article) — and **no source supports an effigy of Fraser.** On 1 September 1977 Benny Ambrose was interviewed on Ottertrack about the fight; the tape is at the Ely district office ([*Minnesota History* 1994](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/54/v54i03p124-137.pdf)).

The settlement was made between two lawyers: Ely City Attorney Ron Walls and environmental attorney Chuck Dayton. Carter signed the **BWCA Wilderness Act on 21 October 1978** ([Wikipedia BWCA Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)). It ended logging, banned most snowmobiling, added more than 50,000 acres (1,098,057 in total), capped motor quotas, and let the government acquire mineral rights in the wilderness and in a Mining Protection Area. **The share of water left open to motors is reported as ~24% (Wikipedia), ~21% (Heinselman article), one-fourth (MinnPost) and 22% (Save the Boundary Waters); the buffer is given as 222,000 or 220,000 acres** ([Save the BW](https://www.savetheboundarywaters.org/updates/boundary-waters-day-history-protecting-wilderness)). The motor side still regards the remaining motor lakes as part of what was promised ([Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)). Minnesota challenged the Act and lost, and the Supreme Court declined review in 1982. **One source records that denial as "8–1," and the challengers and start year (1978 vs 1979) differ** ([Wikipedia Friends of BWW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friends_of_the_Boundary_Waters_Wilderness)). Politically, Fraser lost the September 1978 Senate primary by 3,451 votes to Bob Short, who ran 8th-District ads painting him as a metro elitist. MNopedia attributes Wendell Anderson's loss that year mainly to his "self-appointment" to the Senate, not to the BWCA ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minnesota-massacre-1978-election)). **The claim that Anderson brokered the Senate compromise is unverified.**

### The inholders who would not leave

Two lives give the story an emotional shape. **Dorothy Molter** came to Knife Lake's Isle of Pines in 1930 and lived there from 1934. She was a nurse, and outfitters marked her island on maps as a place to get medical help. After the airban she brewed root beer because bottled pop could no longer be flown in, and she logged thousands of visitors a summer ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/molter-dorothy-1907-1986); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)). **Benny Ambrose**, an Iowa runaway and WWI veteran, prospected and guided from his Ottertrack point from about 1920 until 1982 ([*Minnesota History* 1994](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/54/v54i03p124-137.pdf)). Both properties were condemned around 1964, with the right to stay until 1975. After a public outcry the Forest Service made them "volunteers in service." Supervisor Harold Anderson said he could not be part of throwing them out (same source). **Conflicts:** Northern Wilds dates Molter's lifetime tenancy to 1972, Wikipedia to 1975 ([Northern Wilds](https://northernwilds.com/the-lasting-legacy-of-the-root-beer-lady/)). Her visitor counts vary (about 6,000 a summer; over 4,000 guest-book signatures; 5,000–7,000 a year). *Wilderness News* says she lived there 56 years, which counts from her first visit in 1930, not from 1934. Ambrose's arrival is given as 1919 (Hovland), 1920, or "the 1920s," and his birth year ranges from 1894 to 1899. Their ends differed sharply. Days after Ambrose died, a Forest Service crew burned his buildings and left a rock cairn, stone steps and foundations. Molter's cabins were hauled to Ely by dogsled and snowmobile in 1987 and became a museum ([Northern Wilds](https://northernwilds.com/the-lasting-legacy-of-the-root-beer-lady/)).

## Copper-nickel reopened the 1977 wound and has still dug nothing

The BWCAW sits on the Duluth Complex, a 1.1-billion-year-old gabbro whose northwestern base holds low-grade copper, nickel and platinum-group sulfides ([Wikipedia Duluth Complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duluth_Complex)). **Serious interest followed a discovery in 1948, according to the Legislative Reference Library; Circle of Blue says "the 1950s."** ([LRL guide](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/guides/guides?issue=coppernickel); [Circle of Blue 2026](https://www.circleofblue.org/2026/great-lakes/senate-vote-tests-future-of-boundary-waters-protections/)). In **1966** the federal government granted INCO two leases near Ely, MNES-01352 and -01353. In **May 1974** INCO asked to mine on the South Kawishiwi River just outside the wilderness. Gov. Wendell Anderson opposed it. AMAX won approval for a test site near Babbitt in **1976** ([LRL guide](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/guides/guides?issue=coppernickel)). A 1974 Environmental Quality Council resolution in effect put a moratorium on mining until the **Regional Copper-Nickel Study (1974–79, $4.3 million)** was done. The study concluded that mining could meet standards if technology was used wisely, but warned of risk near existing works. It valued the minerals at $50 billion and projected more than 2,000 jobs. Falling copper prices then drove both companies away. Both sides still quote the study (same source). By law, exploration cores go to the DNR Drill Core Library in Hibbing, first built in 1972. In 2008 its buildings were packed with boxes of two-inch rock cylinders stacked seven deep ([Duluth News Tribune 2008](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/news/drill-cores-are-treasure)); today the collection would stretch from International Falls to Chicago and is open to the public ([WDIO 2026](https://www.wdio.com/lift-online/dnr-drill-core-library-is-unbelievable-resource/)). **The names "Spruce Road" (INCO) and "Minnamax" (AMAX), and a ~1,700 ft test shaft, are unverified background knowledge.**

The 1977 timing matters. The copper-nickel prospect was live in exactly the years when Ely was fighting over motors and logging. A federal court ruling had already barred mining inside the wilderness itself — **Circle of Blue, the only source for it, dates it 1973; no second source confirms it** — and the 1978 Act's mineral clauses tied the two fights together.

The modern fight has swung with each administration. The leases passed to Duluth Metals and then to Twin Metals Minnesota, wholly owned by Chile's Antofagasta from 2014 ([Circle of Blue](https://www.circleofblue.org/2026/great-lakes/senate-vote-tests-future-of-boundary-waters-protections/)). The Obama administration declined to renew them in December 2016, after listening sessions. At Ely's, about 900 people spoke roughly half for and half against ([Wikipedia Twin Metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Metals_mine)). Trump's administration reinstated the leases in 2018 and renewed them on 15 May 2019. **Circle of Blue's "2017" reinstatement conflicts with the LRL, AP and Wikipedia.** Biden's Interior cancelled them on 26 January 2022. **Public Land Order 7917** (26 January 2023) withdrew 225,504 acres for 20 years. Congress overturned the withdrawal with a Congressional Review Act resolution, passing the Senate 50–49 on 16 April 2026; E&E says it is the first such use against a mining withdrawal. Trump signed it on 27 April ([E&E News](https://www.eenews.net/articles/congress-overturns-bidens-boundary-waters-mining-ban/)). The federal leases nonetheless remain cancelled and in litigation, and Interior would have to reissue them. On 18 August 2026 Gov. Walz ordered state agencies to issue no nonferrous permits or leases in about 350 square miles of Rainy River headwaters ([MPR 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/08/18/walz-executive-order-block-copper-mining-near-boundary-waters)). The DNR then cancelled one state lease of about 242 acres, effective 10 November 2026 ([WDIO](https://www.wdio.com/top-stories/minnesota-dnr-cancels-one-of-twin-metals-mineral-leases/)). **The cancellation is dated 11 September by *Wilderness News* and 12–14 September by other outlets; *Wilderness News* alone gives the order number EO 26-10.** ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/dnr-cancels-one-twin-metals-lease-further-protecting-boundary-waters-and-surrounding-watershed/)). Twin Metals' plan, near where the South Kawishiwi enters Birch Lake about 9 miles southeast of Ely, uses dry-stack tailings. **Its job figures differ by source: 700 direct + 1,400 spin-off over 25 years, or ~750 + 1,500 over 25–30 years** ([Wilderness News, Oct 2026](https://queticosuperior.org/recent-poll-shows-most-minnesotans-oppose-new-mines-near-the-boundary-waters/)). South of the divide, the NorthMet project (PolyMet, now NewRange), which drains to Lake Superior, won permits in 2018–19, lost several in court in 2020–23, and in October 2026 was ordered to do a Supplemental EIS ([WDIO](https://www.wdio.com/mining-news/dnr-says-they-will-do-a-supplemental-eis-for-newrange-copper-nickels-project/)). The governor's race on 3 November 2026 decides whether the Walz order stands; the result was not known when the notes were written.

Each side's case deserves a fair statement. **Supporters** argue that mining is the Range's identity and its future, that the country needs domestic copper, nickel and cobalt, that underground mining with dry-stack tailings removes the risk of a dam failure, that a project deserves a full review rather than an executive order, and that union jobs let young people stay. The supporters include Rep. Pete Stauber, Mining Minnesota, United Steelworkers District 11, DFL state Sen. Grant Hauschild, former Ely mayor Rep. Roger Skraba, and an idled Eveleth taconite miner, the son of a Bois Forte member, who wants his children to be able to stay but would not back a mine with excessive risk ([CBS/WCCO](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/boundary-water-mining-debate/); [MPR](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/08/18/walz-executive-order-block-copper-mining-near-boundary-waters)). **Opponents** argue that sulfide ore produces acid drainage at every stage, that this site lies upstream of the wilderness, that the DNR itself has doubted dry stack in a wet climate, and that a review checks numbers rather than asking whether the place is right. They include Becky Rom, who has been the most reviled person in the room at an Ely council meeting; Piragis and other outfitters; retired Soudan miner Bob Tammen, who knows his neighbours' jobs are at stake; a former DNR commissioner who approved NorthMet but opposes this site; and the Ojibwe bands ([Wikipedia Twin Metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Metals_mine); [CBS/WCCO](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/boundary-water-mining-debate/)). A neutral economist doubts the ore is competitive or that the US has the capacity to refine it ([CBS/WCCO](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/does-mine-near-boundary-waters-make-sense/)). Polls show 67% of Minnesotans opposed in October 2026, but northern Minnesota is closer: 56% opposed and 35% in favour, with support up from 22% in 2020 ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/recent-poll-shows-most-minnesotans-oppose-new-mines-near-the-boundary-waters/)). Old resentment carries straight through. A member of the Erzar family called the 1964/78 laws devastating, and Skraba said the bad taste will go away only when he dies ([Wikipedia Twin Metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Metals_mine)). Becky Rom says the mining fight has revived bitter memories of the '50s through the '70s ([Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)).

## Outfitters, guides and searchers became the people who knew the country

Outfitting runs in families, and its family histories follow a common arc. Sigurd Olson's Border Lakes Outfitting in Winton served the Scouts by 1932 ([Wikipedia Northern Tier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Tier_(Scouting))). Bill Rom, a Navy veteran and son of Slovene immigrants, grew Canoe Country Outfitters from 10 wood-canvas canoes in 1946 to 400–600 Grummans. It was North America's largest Grumman dealer, and he answered every mail-order reservation by hand ([Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/)). He sold in 1975 to Bob Olson Sr., who had guided from 1950, worked in the Pioneer mine from 1953 and managed CCO from 1964. That one career joins mine and canoe ([Wikipedia CCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canoe_Country_Outfitters)). Piragis was founded in 1979 by two EPA biologists who had come to Ely in 1975 ([Piragis](https://www.piragis.com/home/about-us.html)). Moose Lake's Williams & Hall descends from Don Beland, an Illinois runaway trapper ([Williams & Hall](https://williamsandhall.com/our-legacy-and-lodge)). Sawbill passed from Frank and Mary Alice Hansen (1957) to Bill and Cindy Hansen in the early 1980s and then to the Shirleys ([Sawbill](https://sawbill.com/about/)). Bill Hansen remembers that losing motors in 1978 cost them towing, repair and rental income, but that paddlers replaced it quickly and that local attitudes were softening with time ([Wilderness News 2006](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-hansen/)). On the Gunflint, lodges came first: Clearwater (1915), Hungry Jack (1924, burned 1931, 1972 and 2008), Gunflint (1925, burned 1953), Bearskin (1925), and Chik-Wauk (1931–1980, bought out in 1978 and now the trail's museum) ([Gunflint Trail Historical Society](https://gunflinthistory.org/about/); [Hungry Jack](https://hungryjacklodge.com/about/history/)). Justine Kerfoot of Gunflint Lodge once took a suspended fine rather than name a Grand Portage customer who had left beaver pelts as collateral ([Wilderness News 2021](https://queticosuperior.org/wilderness-voices-justine-kerfoot-gunflint-trail-leader/)). The Lutheran Wilderness Canoe Base names the founder's family "Blankenberg" where Gunflint Lodge spells it "Blankenburg."

Guiding was a summer trade layered on trapping, mining, teaching or flying. Bill Magie flew a Curtiss Jenny under Duluth's Aerial Bridge on a bet, surveyed the future BWCA by dogsled for the Corps of Engineers in 1925–30, founded Friends of the Wilderness in 1949 and guided until 1979 ([Wikipedia CCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canoe_Country_Outfitters)). He is not the same person as Billy Magee, the Ojibwe guide on Oberholtzer's 1909 trip. A career often ran swamper, then guide, then outfitter staff, then owner. The outfitters' children guided from age 12 (same source).

Searches were a county affair. St. Louis County's volunteer Rescue Squad, founded in 1958, searches for lost canoeists. On the water its main work has long been fatality recovery by dragging and diving; sonar, underwater cameras and cadaver dogs came only recently ([SLCRS](https://www.slcrs.org/about-us)). Search aircraft were always exempt from the airban ([EO 10092](https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/10092/executive-order-10092)), and the Forest Service has flown Beavers out of Ely since 1956 ([Wilderness News 2026](https://queticosuperior.org/beaver-floatplanes-carried-idaho-hotshots-into-boundary-waters-to-fight-bear-trap-fire/)). In the 1999 blowdown, outfitters and guides took motorboats out to search lakes and evacuate the injured ([Wilderness News review](https://queticosuperior.org/book-review-gunflint-falling-by-cary-j-griffith/)). **No dated 1960s–80s BWCA search account was found**, and the notes contain no Minnesota legal standard for declaring a missing person dead.

The disasters gave later generations their shared memories. **The 1999 derecho's size conflicts:** about 370,000 BWCAW acres (Wikipedia) against 478,000 (NWS, via *Wilderness News*), roughly 500,000, or 477,000 (forum); winds of about 90 or 100 mph. Deaths are also disputed: *Wilderness News* says none inside the BWCA and two in Canada, while a forum summary says one person drowned ([Wilderness News 2024](https://queticosuperior.org/fourth-of-july-marks-20th-anniversary-of-boundary-waters-blowdown/); [QuietJourney](https://quietjourney.com/community/YABB.cgi?num=1284168482)). It took nearly two weeks to get everyone out. The Ham Lake fire (May 2007) started at a campsite and burned about 75,000 acres across the border. **Its toll conflicts: "almost 200 structures" (Wikipedia) against 61 residences, 17 commercial buildings and 55 outbuildings (133) on the historical marker; and 36,443 US acres (Wilderness News) against "over 40,000" (Wikipedia)** ([HMDB marker](https://explorehere.app/markers/kh77abhxpqnr9nag9qchmkdcvh7fevec/the-2007-ham-lake-fire)). A Gunflint outfitter who fought it as a volunteer firefighter could not get the sound of propane tanks exploding out of his head ([Boundary Waters Blog](https://www.boundarywatersblog.com/ham-fire-may-19th-2007-part-1/)). At Pagami Creek (2011) the fire ran 16 miles in a day. Six wilderness rangers sheltered in fire shelters on a bare island and in Insula's water, after warnings they say had been ignored, and the Forest Service adopted new protocols afterward ([MPR 2021](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2021/09/10/ten-years-later-lessons-from-the-pagami-creek-fire)). The Greenwood fire (2021) brought the first full BWCA closure "since the 1970s." **Which 1970s closure that refers to is unidentified** ([Wikipedia Greenwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenwood_Fire)). A second full closure began on 14 July 2026. The "July Lightning Incident" burned 71,675 acres and touched about 150 of roughly 2,000 campsites ([Wilderness News, Sept 2026](https://queticosuperior.org/seven-wildfires-burning-in-the-superior-national-forest-and-bwca-after-lightning-storms/)).

What remains in the woods is modest. There are stone foundations, dock footings and stone steps above Curtain Falls ([lodges I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/)); Ambrose's cairn on Ottertrack; mechanical portages at Prairie, Beatty and Loon Falls ([Wilderness News 2025](https://queticosuperior.org/behind-the-mechanical-portages-of-the-bwcaw/)); border markers punched into granite from 1908; and Primitive Management Areas where the Forest Service let old portages go back to brush ([Wilderness News 2025](https://queticosuperior.org/exploring-the-primitive-management-areas-of-the-bwcaw/)). Fire exposes the past. Knife Lake siltstone quarries, possibly 11,000–12,000 years old, were found in 2009 on ground bared by prescribed burns ([Wilderness News 2013](https://queticosuperior.org/archaeologist-says-knife-lake-site-could-be-earliest-minnesota-settlement/)). Forum veterans report rock landing walls on Basswood campsites, garden vegetables going wild where a resort had stood, an abandoned gold-mine trail off the Kelly–Jack portage, and a shovel fished up from 30 feet below Curtain Falls ([bwca.com](https://bwca.com/index.cfm?fuseaction=forum.thread&threadid=475343&confid=1&forumid=12); [bwca.com](https://bwca.com/index.cfm?fuseaction=forum.thread&threadId=1310948&forumID=16&confID=1&vt=1674751687)). These are unverified first-person reports. **Lilacs and rhubarb at resort sites, logging dams, and 1970s drill-pad clearings are commonly reported but unsourced.**

## Conclusion

The history's central lesson for fiction is that loss here was cumulative and arrived in layers. Each new restriction landed on a town already grieving the last one: lands ceded, then logging gone, then planes grounded, then resorts burned on the ice, then the mine closed, then motors restricted, then a mine that might bring the work back blocked. So the anger at the 1977 Ely hearing was not about canoes alone. It was about a town told for the fifth time in thirty years that outsiders would decide its future. The other side's grief was just as real: people who had watched a dam plan, fly-in resorts and logging roads come within a vote of ending the canoe country, and who believed the place was permanent wealth and that mining was not. The lines between the two were never neat. The Canoe King was a miner's son, a former Pioneer miner bought his outfitter, a resort daughter came to be glad of the buyout, and a retired miner opposes the copper mine. That cross-cutting is the most truthful, and the most dramatic, thing the record offers.

The record also has holes in exactly the places a mystery needs them. There is no documented 1970s search, no described drill camp, no named draft resister and no "Operation Snowbird." A story can fill those holes freely, so long as it says it is fiction and keeps the surrounding calendar accurate.

## What this means for the story

**Real ground the fiction can stand on.** The 1977 frame sits on a dense, sourced calendar. The airban and the confiscated planes of 1952 are a generation old. The Basswood resorts closed between 1964 and 1970. The Pioneer Mine closed on 1 April 1967. Canoe Country Outfitters was picketed and sold in 1975. INCO's South Kawishiwi application dates from May 1974 and AMAX's Babbitt test site from 1976, both frozen under the state's 1974–79 copper-nickel study. Heinselman's Friends was founded in May 1976 and the Eighth Circuit reinstated logging in August 1976. The Ely hearing of 8 July 1977 — trucks, effigy, minutes of booing — is the strongest real scene available, and a dead guide's notebook could plausibly record being in that hall. A fictional guide in 1977 would plausibly be Slovene or Finnish Ely stock, guide in summer and work in the mines or trap in winter, use a Grumman, Duluth packs and rods for portage lengths, and drink straight from the lake. They would know the inholders, the sound of the snowmobile routes, and Prairie Portage, where Ely parties crossed into Quetico, paid fees and bought licences. The real places — Basswood's resort shores, the Curtain Falls stone steps, the Four Mile Portage grade, Knife Lake, Ottertrack's cairn, the Hibbing core library, Moose Lake's tow docks, the Gunflint lodges that burned and were rebuilt — can all appear as settings without naming real private people as characters.

A period-true search can be built from documented parts. The family or the outfitter reports the party overdue. The sheriff calls out the volunteer rescue squad. Forest Service Beavers and local pilots fly the route. Outfitters search by motorboat where motors were still allowed. Once a swamped canoe is found, the work turns to blind dragging and diving in dark water. "Presumed drowned" resting on an empty canoe and an unrecovered body is a plausible inference, but the legal process should be invented and kept vague. For the present-day frame (the game's November 2026), the real situation is that nothing has been mined after sixty years of fighting. The federal withdrawal has been repealed, but the leases are still cancelled and in court. A governor's order blocks state permits and one state lease is cancelled from 10 November. The election result decides what happens next. In the woods, the 2026 lightning fires burned about 150 campsites, and fire is a documented way for buried things to come to light. Canada's Remote Area Border Crossing permit ended on 14 September 2026.

**Where the fiction must invent, and should say so.** No documented case exists of a Vietnam-era draft resister crossing through the BWCA or Quetico. Recorded routes went by road and air, so a resister going "out through the woods" is fiction, though the 1842 open-portage rule and lightly staffed crossings make it plausible. "Operation Snowbird" is unverified and should be used, if at all, as an invented local nickname. The 1977 disappearance, its search, the notebooks, any drill-camp or core-shed scene, the names "Spruce Road" and "Minnamax," and any role for Wendell Anderson are invented or unverified. Any resort family in the story should be fictional, not Perko, Skala or Zupancich. The real inholders (Molter, Ambrose) can be honoured by fictional analogues with the same arc of refusal, condemnation, outcry and the "volunteer" reprieve, without the story putting words in their mouths or crimes near them. Real public figures (Olson, Heinselman, Oberstar, Vento, Rom) can appear only as offstage voices at real public events.

**Ethical lines.** Keep Ojibwe sacred material out entirely: no pictograph meanings, no spirit beings, no use of the Lac La Croix leaders' description of Quetico Lake as sacred, no ceremonies. Ojibwe characters should be people with jobs, legal grievances and treaty rights, drawn from the documented work (guiding, logging, resort staff, rice and berries) and from the documented grievances: the off-reservation hunting treated as poaching until 1988, and the reserve taken for Quetico. Real tragedies should be used by shape only. A Pagami-style entrapment, a blowdown campsite death or a solo capsize can inspire a scene, but no real victim — including Gov. Walz's brother, who died in a campsite blowdown — should be identifiable. On mining, the story should be even-handed in structure, not just in lines. The pro-mining character needs the strongest true arguments (a town's survival, union wages, domestic metals, a right to review) and personal decency, and the wilderness character needs real costs (the neighbour's job, the summer-only economy). Neither side should own the villain. The documented cross-cutters — the miner's son who outfits, the miner who opposes, the Ojibwe miner who supports — are the model.

**Grounded reasons the player came north and cares about the notebooks.** Each option below uses a pattern the record documents.

| Option | Why it is grounded | Why the notebooks matter |
|---|---|---|
| Grandchild of a 1960s Basswood resort family whose lodge was bought and burned on the ice | Buyouts and ice burnings are documented, and so is later acceptance by resort families | The guide worked that resort, and the notebooks hold the family's last season and who sold out to whom |
| Child of a Pioneer miner who moved away after 1967 | Mine closure and population loss are sourced | The guide was the parent's mine partner in winter; the 1977 entry names the parent |
| New owner of a small family outfitter bought from an ageing couple | The Iowa/Illinois couple buying an Ely outfitter is the standard documented arc | The notebooks came with the building, behind the canoe racks |
| Seasonal crew member or Northern Tier interpreter | Staffing shortages and crew culture are documented | A returning crew alumnus hands them over at a reunion |
| Geologist or contractor reviewing old core in Hibbing for a 2026 exploration plan | The core library and the 2026 drilling filings are real | A 1970s core-box label in the guide's handwriting links him to an exploration camp |
| Paddler on a post-fire trip in the 2026 burn | The 2026 fires, closures and the "fire bares the past" pattern are real | A cache exposed at a burned campsite holds the notebooks |
| Descendant of a 1977 Twin Cities wilderness volunteer who came to the Ely hearing | Metro volunteers at the hearing are documented | The missing person was the volunteer's friend, and the notebooks record what the guide saw |

The strongest option ties the player to both sides at once — for example, a mining-family parent and an outfitter-family grandparent — so that reading the notebooks forces the player to hear each side's grief in the people who held it.
