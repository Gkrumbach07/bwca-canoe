## 1. Ojibwe presence, the 1854 and 1866 treaties, retained rights, and the effects of later policy

### Takeaway
The Bois Forte and Grand Portage bands (with Fond du Lac) ceded the Arrowhead in the 1854 Treaty of La Pointe. Article 11 kept their right to "hunt and fish therein". In 1866 the Bois Forte gave up the undefined Lake Vermilion reserve, right after the Vermilion gold rush, in exchange for Nett Lake. The state disregarded these off-reservation rights for most of the 20th century; they were reaffirmed only in the 1985–88 Grand Portage v. Minnesota settlement. On the Canadian side, Ontario's creation of Quetico took the Sturgeon Lake reserve (24C) from the Lac La Croix people, now Gakijiwanong Anishinaabe Nation. The province formally apologised in 1991.

### Cited Findings
**Before the treaties**
- Archaeology: the region has been occupied since about 8000 BC (Paleo-Indian), with later Archaic and Late Woodland pottery and clay pipes. Wikipedia says the area was "sparsely populated by the Sioux" before the Ojibwe arrived. That is a simplification. The Forest Service lists Dakota, Cree, Assiniboine and Ojibwe in the contact period — [Wikipedia BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness); [USFS Superior NF heritage page (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856)
- The Forest Service's history page quotes Alexander Mackenzie (late 1700s) on Basswood Lake. Before the 1780 smallpox epidemic the population there "was very numerous", and Basswood was "a favourite part, where they made their canoes" — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856)
- A Forest Service photo caption reads: "1903 Ojibwe Indian village on Basswood Lake near Washington Island. Chief Blackstone in foreground." Another (c. 1900) shows families building birchbark canoes, with Chief Blackstone gumming a canoe — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856)
- Grand Portage has been the Ojibwe "Gichi Onigaming" ("Great Carrying Place") for perhaps 2,000 years. In 1729 the Cree guide Auchagah drew a route map for La Vérendrye — [NPS Grand Portage History & Culture](https://www.nps.gov/grpo/learn/historyculture/index.htm); [Wikipedia Grand Portage NM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument)
- The Bois Forte name in Ojibwe is Zagaakwaandagowininiwag, "Men of the Thick Fir-woods". The band merged three groups: the Bois Forte proper, the Lake Vermilion Band, and the southern half of the Little Forks (Rainy River) band — [Wikipedia Bois Forte Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bois_Forte_Band_of_Chippewa). The band's own site says "Bois Forte, or 'strong wood'" was the French name for people of the densest forests — [boisforte.com history](https://boisforte.com/about/history/)
- Border Lakes Ojibwe used planned fire at regular intervals to keep blueberries and medicinal plants, reduce ticks, and make habitat for moose. Red pine fire scars show a fire every 10–20 years from the 1600s to 1905, followed by "nine decades of fire suppression" — [USFS "A Long History of Fire" (2026)](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r09/superior/newsroom/stories/long-history-fire)

**Treaty of La Pointe, 30 September 1854 (10 Stat. 1109)**
- US negotiators were Henry C. Gilbert and David B. Herriman. Thousands of Ojibwe attended. Ojibwe negotiators included Bizhiki (Buffalo). The treaty was signed by 85 leaders on September 30 and ratified by Congress on January 10, 1855 (Wikipedia gives proclamation as January 29, 1855) — [MNopedia Treaty of La Pointe](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/treaty-la-pointe-1854); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_La_Pointe)
- The US aim was copper, minerals and pine. Many Ojibwe were in debt to traders, and the treaty added $90,000 for "present just engagements" (those debts) — [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/treaty-la-pointe-1854); [Kappler text, OSU](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-1854-0648)
- Article 11, verbatim: "such of them as reside in the territory hereby ceded, shall have the right to hunt and fish therein, until otherwise ordered by the President." It also promised the Ojibwe "shall not be required to remove" — [Kappler text](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-1854-0648); [1854 Treaty Authority](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/about/who-we-are)
- Why the bands insisted on staying: the 1850 Sandy Lake Tragedy. Annuity payments were moved to Sandy Lake, the agent arrived in late November with meagre supplies, and "hundreds died" walking home through winter — [1854 Treaty Authority booklet "The Right to Hunt and Fish Therein" (PDF)](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/images/ToHuntandFish.updated2020.pdf); [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/treaty-la-pointe-1854)
- The Grand Portage reservation was described "Beginning at a rock a little east of the eastern extremity of Grand Portage Bay". The treaty also set special stipulations "in consideration of the poverty of the Bois Forte Indians", who had never received annuities — [Kappler](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-1854-0648)
- The treaty also supplied guns, ammunition and beaver traps. The Treaty Authority reads this as proof that both sides expected hunting to continue across the whole ceded area — [1854TA booklet](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/images/ToHuntandFish.updated2020.pdf)

**Bois Forte treaty, 7 April 1866 (14 Stat. 765)**
- Signed in Washington, DC by Commissioner D. N. Cooley and E. E. L. Taylor. The Bois Forte signers included Gabeshcodaway (Going through the Prairie), Babawmadjeweshcang (Mountain Traveller), Neoning (The Four Fingers) and Gawnandawawinzo (Berry Hunter) — [Kappler 1866](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-bois-fort-band-1866-0916)
- Article 2: the band ceded all its lands, "more especially" the area "at and near Lake Vermillion as a reservation". Article 3 promised at least 100,000 acres including Nett Lake ("Netor As-sab-a-co-na") and a township at Deer Creek, plus a blacksmith shop, a school, eight chiefs' houses, and an $11,000 annual annuity — [Kappler 1866](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-bois-fort-band-1866-0916)
- Article 7 says inconsistent former treaties are "abrogated and made void". The 1866 text says nothing on hunting or fishing — [Kappler 1866](https://treaties.okstate.edu/treaties/treaty-with-the-chippewa-bois-fort-band-1866-0916)
- Context: the Vermilion Lake gold rush came in 1865, with specks of gold in quartz. It was over by 1866–67, but the prospectors reported iron — [Wikipedia Vermilion Lake gold rush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermilion_Lake_gold_rush); [Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)
- The Vermilion sector was defined by an 1881 executive order and Deer Creek by one in 1883. Today the Vermilion sector holds Fortune Bay casino (opened August 1986) — [Bois Forte history](https://boisforte.com/about/history/); [1854TA booklet](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/images/ToHuntandFish.updated2020.pdf)
- The Grand Portage reservation was "abolished" in 1887 and opened to settlement, according to Wikipedia. That claim needs checking; the reservation exists today — [Wikipedia Grand Portage NM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument)

**Rights eroded, then reasserted**
- In the late 1800s and early 1900s, allotment and boarding schools coincided with state tourism interest. This led to "illegal extension of state jurisdiction" over Indian hunting and fishing (the Treaty Authority's own words) — [1854TA booklet](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/images/ToHuntandFish.updated2020.pdf)
- The Forest Service says that in 1854 and 1866 the bands kept "off-reservation hunting and gathering rights". Ojibwe men worked in logging: John Linklater and Henry Chosa ran equipment on Hoist Bay of Basswood for Swallow & Hopkins. Frank Powell and Freddie Douillard guided for Saganaga resorts. Women sold wild rice, blueberries and maple syrup to tourists — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856)
- The modern case: in 1984 a Grand Portage member was cited for a moose. The band sued in 1985, and Fond du Lac and Bois Forte joined. A 1988 memorandum of agreement with Minnesota followed, which created the Tri-Band Authority, now the 1854 Treaty Authority (Fond du Lac withdrew the next year) — [1854TA booklet](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/images/ToHuntandFish.updated2020.pdf); [1854TA who we are](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/about/who-we-are)
- Band members need no BWCAW permit when exercising treaty rights, but must carry an 1854 ID — [1854TA FAQ](https://www.1854treatyauthority.org/1854-faq-s)

**Canada: Lac La Croix / Gakijiwanong and Quetico**
- The band signed Treaty 3. It renamed itself Gakijiwanong Anishinaabe Nation ("where the water flows") in 2023. Its reserve is Neguagon Lake IR 25D. It once also held Sturgeon Lake IR 24C, which became part of Quetico — [Wikipedia Gakijiwanong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac_La_Croix_First_Nation)
- The date is disputed. One Wikipedia page says Ontario "cancelled the band's right to Reserve 24C" in 1915 and relocated the people. The band's own page says the Provincial Parks Act of 1950 took it. In 1991 Minister Bud Wildman made a "landmark, formal apology" in the legislature — [Wikipedia Quetico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetico_Provincial_Park); contradicted on date by [Wikipedia Gakijiwanong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lac_La_Croix_First_Nation)
- Lac La Croix guides kept motorboat rights (10 hp or less) on certain Quetico lakes under the "Agreement of Coexistence" (post-1960 context) — [Wikipedia Quetico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetico_Provincial_Park)
- Conservation "coincided with the loss of Indigenous lands". The 1887 Dawes Act broke up communal land — [Wilderness News, women conservationists (2025)](https://queticosuperior.org/women-who-shaped-minnesotas-early-conservation-movement/)
- Oberholtzer felt the 1940s fight over fly-ins "overlooked the needs of the native population", and stepped back to Rainy Lake — [Wilderness News on Oberholtzer](https://queticosuperior.org/ernest-c-oberholtzer-lifetime-advocate/)

### Inferences
- For story-timing, the 1866 treaty, the Vermilion gold rush and the later Soudan/Tower iron start (1882–84) form a single sequence: gold rumour → treaty cession of Vermilion → iron towns on that land.
- A 1940s–50s Ojibwe character would credibly be working as a guide, a logging hand or a resort worker, or selling rice and berries. Their off-reservation hunting would have been in legal limbo, often treated as poaching by state wardens (rights not reaffirmed until 1988).

### Gaps
- No primary source found for the exact Bois Forte (Nett Lake) population or living conditions in the 1900–1960 era.
- Whether the 1866 treaty's "abrogated" clause affected Bois Forte 1854 hunting rights is a legal question. The 1988 agreement treats the rights as intact for both bands, but I found no court ruling on it.
- MNopedia says the Voight decision (1983) upheld these rights. Voight is a Wisconsin case under the 1837/1842 treaties; it is not the 1854 Minnesota ruling. Treat MNopedia's wording as loose.

## 2. Fur trade and the voyageur route

### Takeaway
From the 1730s (La Vérendrye) to 1803, Grand Portage on Lake Superior was the summer rendezvous of the Montreal trade. The North West Company (formed in the mid-1780s) had its depot and Great Hall there, and Fort Charlotte stood at the far end of the 8.5-mile carry. The Nor'Westers' border route ran: Pigeon River → Rose Lake → Height of Land Portage (South to North Lake, the Laurentian Divide) → Gunflint → Saganaga → Knife → Basswood → Crooked → Lac La Croix. After the US border dispute, the NWC moved to Fort William in 1802–03. The 1842 Webster–Ashburton Treaty fixed the border on this route and kept its portages "free and open" to both countries.

### Cited Findings
- 1688: Jacques de Noyon became the first European known in the area, using the Kaministiquia–Dog–Maligne route (later the "Prairie Portage" route to Lac La Croix). The 1730s: La Vérendrye opened regular trade. 1732: he "used native routes" to reach Rainy Lake — [Wikipedia BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness); [Wikipedia Height of Land Portage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_of_Land_Portage)
- Grand Portage was one of the British Empire's four main fur centres (with Niagara, Detroit and Michilimackinac). The NPS says that "Between 1731 and 1804, thousands of men" shuttled goods over the trail — [Wikipedia GPNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument); [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/grpo/learn/historyculture/index.htm)
- In mid-July 1802 the NWC partners, meeting in the Great Hall, voted to move headquarters to the mouth of the Kaministiquia (Fort William). The final Grand Portage rendezvous was 1803. The NWC merged into the HBC in 1821 — [Wikipedia GPNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument); [Wikipedia BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness)
- The route west, as recorded by NWC clerk John McDonell (1790s) and Henry Hind (1860), went up the Pigeon from Fort Charlotte. The heavy canoes took a cut-off over the Petit Neuf (Watap) Portage to Watap Lake, the Petit Détroit to Rove Lake, and the Grand Neuf (Long) Portage to Rose Lake. From there it ran up the Arrow to South Lake, over Height of Land to North Lake, and west to Lac La Croix, about 100 miles west of Grand Portage — [Wikipedia GPNM "Route west"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument)
- Height of Land Portage crosses the Laurentian Divide between South Lake (Arrow → Pigeon → Superior) and North Lake (Rainy → Winnipeg → Hudson Bay). Its length is given as 80 rods (about 400 m) in one Wikipedia article and "700-yard" in another (a discrepancy). There was once a refitting station for canoe repair at its west end — [Wikipedia Height of Land Portage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_of_Land_Portage); [Wikipedia GPNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument)
- Initiation of new men at Height of Land: a newcomer was sprinkled with a cedar bough dipped in water and swore not to let a novice pass uninitiated, and never to kiss another voyageur's wife without her consent. This was followed by a gun salute and "high wine". The new man became a "homme du nord" or Nor'Wester — [Wikipedia Height of Land Portage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_of_Land_Portage)
- Labour: the standard pièce was a 90-lb pack, and men carried two per carry, sometimes more. They kept 14-hour days at about 55 strokes per minute, and measured distance in "pipes". Hernias, drowning and broken limbs were common. The Montreal-to-Grand-Portage men were the "mangeurs de lard" (pork eaters). The canot de maître was about 36 ft, ~600 lb, with 65 pièces and 6–12 crew — [Wikipedia Voyageurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyageurs)
- Short quote, a retired voyageur to James H. Baker: "no portage was ever too long for me, fifty songs could I sing" — [Wikipedia Voyageurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyageurs)
- Debt: "nine hundred men owed [the company] more than ten to fifteen years pay" (quoted secondhand) — [Wilderness News, Magical History Tour](https://queticosuperior.org/magical-history-tour-through-the-bwca/)
- The NWC reached Lake Vermilion. Later British–American posts (American Fur Co., Northern Lake Co.) ran on the border lakes until about 1870. Fur-trade sites on Saganaga Lake were excavated by Passport in Time volunteers in 2009 and judged eligible for the National Register — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856); [USFS PIT (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049870)
- Webster–Ashburton (9 August 1842): "all the usual portages along the line from Lake Superior to the Lake of the Woods; and also Grand Portage ... shall be free and open". Britain had argued for the St. Louis–Vermilion route and the US for the Kam–Dog–Maligne route — [Wilderness News on the border](https://queticosuperior.org/how-is-the-international-border-marked-in-the-bwca/); [Wikipedia Height of Land Portage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_of_Land_Portage)
- The Ojibwe and French were "mutually dependent". The Ojibwe supplied pelts, winter food, canoes and snowshoes; the French supplied guns, cloth, copper kettles and tobacco — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049856)
- Grand Portage became a National Historic Site in 1951 and a National Monument in 1958 (710 acres, about half donated by the band) — [Wikipedia GPNM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Portage_National_Monument); [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/grpo/learn/historyculture/index.htm)

### Inferences
- The game's border lakes (Saganaga, Knife, Basswood, Crooked, Lac La Croix) were the NWC highway. Old portages there are "the usual portages" the 1842 treaty protected, which is why some trails cross the line.

### Gaps
- I did not find primary data on Rose Falls, or exact NWC post locations on Basswood or Lac La Croix.
- "Fort Charlotte" as a stand-alone article was not found on Wikipedia. NPS describes it as now a campsite marked by a small river-stone monument — [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/grpo/learn/historyculture/index.htm)

## 3. The logging era (1890s–1940s)

### Takeaway
Big-pine logging reached the border lakes in the 1890s, after Michigan and Wisconsin were cut out. It ran on river drives, ice roads and logging railroads, and switched to pulpwood after 1929. On the Ely–Winton side, Swallow & Hopkins ran rail over the Four Mile Portage (Fall Lake to Hoist Bay on Basswood), ferried locomotives on scows, and milled at Winton; the St. Croix Lumber company also milled at Winton. On the Gunflint side, the General Logging Co. pushed the old Alger-Smith railroad to Rose Lake in the late 1920s, until the 1929 Brule Lake fire and the Depression stopped it. Traces remain: the Four Mile Portage rail grade (rails pulled 1918), the Bull-of-the-Woods scow in Burntside Lake, and resort-era rail portages.

### Cited Findings
- "Big pine timber logging began on the Superior National Forest in the 1890's and continued into the 1920s." River drives came first, then railroad logging, plus winter ice roads. "Logging after 1929 focused more and more on pulp species" — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)
- Many homestead claims (1869–1900) "were fraudulently claimed" to get timber for lumber companies — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)
- George Good, bookkeeper for the Swallow and Hopkins Lumber Company, "had workers build scows on which to ferry railroad cars and engines across the waterways", pulled by steamers, from the late 1800s into the 1900s — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049821)
- Swallow & Hopkins "is said to have hauled 350 million board feet of pine over the Four Mile Portage over 10 years" (hedged in the source) — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/magical-history-tour-through-the-bwca/)
- The Four Mile Portage was built so logs could reach Winton mills. "The Four-Mile portage railway ... was dismantled in 1918" — [Wilderness News, Basswood Beach](https://queticosuperior.org/basswood-beach-resort-canoe-trips/); [Wilderness News, mechanical portages](https://queticosuperior.org/behind-the-mechanical-portages-of-the-bwcaw/)
- Winton's post office was established in 1895, named for lumberman William C. Winton. The 2000 census still shows 17.8% Finnish and 8.6% Slovene ancestry — [Wikipedia Winton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winton,_Minnesota)
- The first BSA canoe expedition (Northern Tier's ancestor) launched "from Winton, MN at St. Croix Lumber and Manufacturing Company". It became a BSA Region 10 program in 1926 — [Wikipedia Northern Tier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Tier_(Scouting))
- The Bull-of-the-Woods logging scow, built around 1893, is a small paddle steamer ("alligator") wrecked in Burntside Lake. These boats could tow log rafts and "pull themselves across dry land", and this is the only known survivor. It is on the National Register — [Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)
- Major logging around Ely "virtually disappeared by 1920". Underground mines needed timbering, which drove the early cutting — [Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)
- Gunflint side: "Rose Lake became the terminus of the Alger Smith railroad after the General Logging Company purchased its northern portion" (1920s). Logging reached Rose Lake around 1929, then ended after the Brule Lake Fire of 1929 and the Depression — [Wikipedia Rose Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rose_Lake_(Pigeon_River)); [Wikipedia Brule Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brule_Lake_(Minnesota))
- The General Logging Co. was cutting second growth in the Brule and Cascade valleys from 1928. Alger-Smith began cutting in 1898 (the Tettegouche camp) — [Wikipedia Judge C. R. Magney SP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge_C._R._Magney_State_Park); [Wilderness News, Tettegouche](https://queticosuperior.org/winter-magic-at-the-historic-tettegouche-camp/)
- In 1936 Frank Hubachek bought a Basswood tract to stop the clearcut of "one of the last stands of old-growth timber". It became the Wilderness Research Center, built by "a crew of Finns" — [Wilderness News, Hub's Place](https://queticosuperior.org/hubs-place-wilderness-research-center/)
- Mechanical portages from logging and fishing still exist. Beatty Portage (1913, Lac La Croix–Little Loon) was used by commercial fishermen; the 1928 boundary adjustment moved it to the US. Loon Falls had a rail portage built by commercial fishermen in the early 1900s — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/behind-the-mechanical-portages-of-the-bwcaw/)
- Shipstead-Newton-Nolan (1930) banned logging within 400 ft of shorelines, after which "logging industries went into decline" in the border lakes — [Wilderness News, historic lodges I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/)
- The 1948 roadless boundary changes deleted land near Isabella and Kawishiwi lakes for logging needs — [Wikipedia Dorothy Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)

### Inferences
- A 1910s scene could show steam scows hauling a locomotive across Fall Lake, Ojibwe and immigrant crews on Hoist Bay, and log rafts towed to Winton. A 1930s scene would show cutover land, pulp camps, and former loggers turning to resorts. The Wilderness News lodge history explicitly says ex-loggers and trappers built the first fishing camps.

### Gaps
- Specific St. Croix Lumber dates (mill opening and closing) and the exact Swallow & Hopkins operating years were not confirmed from a primary source.
- Named dams and dam remnants (e.g. Kawishiwi logging dams, the Basswood River) were not found. Knife Lake logging camps are also unconfirmed. The USFS page referencing a "Knife Lake" presentation had no content.

## 4. The iron range towns: Tower, Soudan, Ely, Winton, Babbitt

### Takeaway
Tower and Soudan (1882–84) were Minnesota's first iron towns, and the Soudan mine went underground and ran until 1962. Ely followed in 1888 when the railroad arrived. The Chandler mine came first, then the Pioneer (1889), the most productive and closed in 1967. The workforce was overwhelmingly immigrant: Finnish, Slovene/"Austrian", Italian, Cornish captains, Scandinavian. Labour history centres on the 1907 and 1916 strikes (mainly the Mesabi, with Finnish blacklisting after 1907). Babbitt was a postwar taconite company town (from 1944, Reserve Mining), shipping to Silver Bay from 1955.

### Cited Findings
- 1882: Charlemagne Tower and Samuel Munson incorporated the Minnesota Iron Company. Tower was "the first mining town on the range". The Two Harbors–Tower railroad was built in 1884, and mining reached Ely in 1888 — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)
- Tower was incorporated on March 13, 1889 ("oldest city in the Arrowhead") and named for Charlemagne Tower — [Wikipedia Tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower,_Minnesota)
- Soudan: the open pit began in 1882 and went underground by 1900. It was owned 1901–1962 by U.S. Steel's Oliver Iron Mining. Ore was often more than 65% iron. It was 1,250 ft deep by 1912, with a lowest level of 27 (2,341 ft). Mining used "cut and fill". Active mining ended in 1962 — [Wikipedia Soudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soudan_Underground_Mine_State_Park)
- Ely: the DM&IR rails reached it in 1888 and the Chandler Mine opened. The first town was "Florence" (pop. 177), named for the daughter of Chandler Captain Jack Pengilly, the first mayor; it was renamed for Samuel B. Ely. The Pioneer opened in 1889, followed by the Zenith (1892), Savoy and Sibley (1899), 11 mines in all. The Pioneer produced 41 million tons, about 40% of the Vermilion Range output, and closed in 1967 — [Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)
- Early Ely: Father Buh came from Tower to say the first Mass. Tanner's Hospital (1901) was built because of "the area's high disease rate", a consequence of poor sanitation in boom towns. The Ely State Theater dates from 1936. The post office murals *Iron-Ore Mines* and *Wilderness* were painted by Elsa Jemne (1941) — [Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)
- Immigrant lives: John Jaeger was a Slovenian immigrant architect. His Wolf Island cabin on Vermilion (1927–29) was built "by Jaeger and Finnish American craftsmen from the Tower area". He saw Bois Forte people building canoes on the island — [USFS Wolf Island (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fseprd1162355)
- Bill Rom's parents immigrated "from Yugoslavia, now Slovenia, in the late 1800s"; he was born near Ely in 1917 — [Wikipedia Canoe Country Outfitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canoe_Country_Outfitters)
- 1907 strike (Mesabi): the WFM's organiser Teofilo Petriella set up Italian, Slavic and Finnish sections. Strikers demanded an eight-hour day, safety and a minimum wage. 10,000–16,000 struck from 20 July. Finns were blacklisted, falling from 18% to 8% of miners — [Wikipedia 1907 strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1907_Mesabi_Range_strike)
- 1916 strike: it began on June 2 with Joe Greeni at Aurora. The IWW organised, demanding an end to contract (piece) pay. More than 1,000 armed guards were hired. Rents were 20% above the Twin Cities and food 50–100% higher — [Wikipedia 1916 strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1916_Mesabi_Range_strike)
- Babbitt "grew out of the formation of a taconite mine built by Armco and Republic Steel starting in 1944". Silver Bay was founded on May 1, 1954 as the processing town, 60 miles away by rail. Reserve began discharging tailings into Lake Superior in 1955 — [Wikipedia Babbitt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babbitt,_Minnesota); [Wikipedia Silver Bay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_Bay,_Minnesota); [Wikipedia Taconite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taconite)
- Ely's tourism: the town "built an airport, golf course"; by the late 1940s it was the largest freshwater seaplane base in the continental US, with up to 38 seaplane flights over Ely a day — [Wilderness News, historic lodges I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/)
- The Paulson Mine near Gunflint Lake (exploration from 1886) was linked in 1892 by the Port Arthur, Duluth & Western Railroad. It collapsed in the Panic of 1893, and the railroad was abandoned by 1914 "after hauling only a single load of ore" — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)
- Ely's 1910-era Prohibition footnote: ex-St. Paul police chief Thomas A. Brown, tied to the Dillinger and Barker-Karpis gangs, later ran a liquor store in Ely — [Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)

### Inferences
- The Perko, Skala and Zupancich resort owners in the airban case have Slovenian-sounding names, which fits Ely's Slovene community. This is an inference, not documented.

### Gaps
- Chandler mine closing date not found (common memory says the 1940s; unverified).
- Vermilion-range-specific strike events (Ely or Soudan in 1907 or 1916) were not found. The strikes are documented for the Mesabi.
- No dedicated source found on Ely's Slovenian, Finnish and Italian halls, churches or co-ops.

## 5. Protection: 1909 to the airban and the resort buyouts

### Takeaway
Gen. C. C. Andrews withdrew land from 1902 to 1908. Superior National Forest (13 February 1909) and the Quetico Forest and Game Reserve (1 April 1909) followed, and Quetico Provincial Park in 1913. Carhart's 1919–22 recreation plan led to Secretary Jardine's 640,000-acre roadless area in 1926. Edward Backus's 1925 plan for seven border dams (raising some lakes up to 80 ft, by one claim) prompted Oberholtzer, Hubachek, Kelly and Tyng to form the Quetico-Superior Council. The result was the Shipstead-Newton-Nolan Act (10 July 1930): no homesteading, no change in water levels, no logging within 400 ft of shore. Postwar fly-in resorts boomed (16–20+ on Basswood alone). Thye-Blatnik (1948, $500,000) began buyouts. Truman's Executive Order 10092 (17 December 1949) closed the airspace below 4,000 ft from 1 January 1951, with private landowners exempt until 1 January 1952. Resort owners Perko, Skala and Zupancich and Ely pilot West defied it and lost in court (1952; 8th Cir. 1953). The name "Boundary Waters Canoe Area" came in 1958.

### Cited Findings
- Andrews secured three withdrawals from 1902 to 1908 and urged Ontario to match. TR's Proclamation No. 848 (13 February 1909) created the Superior NF (about 1,018,638 acres per Wilderness News; the USFS says 644,114 acres originally — a discrepancy). Ontario's Order-in-Council of 1 April 1909 created a 1,148,000-acre reserve "to be preserved and set apart as a public park and forest reserve, fish and game preserve" — [Wilderness News, 100 years](https://queticosuperior.org/quetico-superior-region-100-years-stewardship-beyond-boundaries/); [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)
- Andrews' vision was "an international forest reserve and park of very great beauty and interest" — [Wikipedia BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness)
- Quetico became a provincial park in 1913. Road access came only in 1954 — [Wikipedia Quetico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetico_Provincial_Park)
- Arthur Carhart and Ranger Matt Soderbeck (also spelled Soderback) made two 24-day canoe surveys in 1919 and 1921. Carhart urged a "reasonable belt of timber along the edge of the water" and reported in 1922 — [Wilderness News, old photos](https://queticosuperior.org/old-photos-provide-peek-history/); [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)
- 1926: Jardine set aside 640,000 acres, zoned for cars, boats and canoes. The Ely–Gunflint road was not to be built — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/old-photos-provide-peek-history/); [Wikipedia BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness). In 1952 the court dated the first roadless area to "1927 or thereabouts" — [US v. Perko, 108 F. Supp. 315](https://static.case.law/f-supp/108/cases/0315-01.json)
- Backus (1861–1934) ran Ontario & Minnesota Power and Minnesota and Ontario Paper, and built the Koochiching Falls dam (from 1905). His 1925 plan called for seven dams; he was overextended in the Depression and died in 1934 — [Wikipedia Backus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Wellington_Backus). Wilderness News says the dams would have raised water "80 feet, submerging Lac la Croix's islands", with no falls left at Basswood Falls. That figure is unverified and may be an exaggeration — [Wilderness News on Oberholtzer](https://queticosuperior.org/ernest-c-oberholtzer-lifetime-advocate/)
- Oberholtzer (1884–1977) made his "3,000-mile summer" in 1909 with Ojibwe guide Dedaabaswewidang (Billy Magee). He lived on Mallard Island (Rainy Lake), and became president of the Quetico-Superior Council (1925 onward) — [MNopedia Oberholtzer](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/oberholtzer-ernest-1884-1977). Note: Billy **Magee** (Ojibwe, Rainy Lake) is a different person from Bill **Magie** (Ely guide).
- Oberholtzer's quote: "Common sense counsels me to drop the whole struggle… Common sense is a very small part of my make-up." His 1957 line: "seen a whole pioneer period pass—probably the last" — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/ernest-c-oberholtzer-lifetime-advocate/)
- The Minneapolis men (Frank Hubachek and Charlie Kelly among them) first "met in secret" in a basement — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/ernest-c-oberholtzer-lifetime-advocate/); [Hub's Place](https://queticosuperior.org/hubs-place-wilderness-research-center/)
- Shipstead-Newton-Nolan was signed by Hoover in 1930 (10 July 1930 per the court) and covers federal land in the Rainy Lake watershed. Minnesota extended similar rules to state land in 1933. FDR's President's Quetico-Superior Committee followed in 1934. In 1938 the area was renamed the Superior Roadless Primitive Area — [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/oberholtzer-ernest-1884-1977); [US v. Perko](https://static.case.law/f-supp/108/cases/0315-01.json); [Wikipedia BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness)
- Resorts: there were more than 40 resorts in the future BWCAW by the early 1940s, employing over 300. Named examples:
  - Basswood: Basswood Lodge (begun 1921, 100+ guests, two daily seaplanes), Pipestone Falls Lodge, Skidway Borderline, Pinecliff, Johnson Bros., Evergreen, Peterson Fishing Camp
  - Curtain Falls Fishing Camp on Crooked Lake, reached only by seaplane and "rumored to have had a slot machine"
  - Wegen's Wilderness Camp on Newton Lake (from 1924)
  
  — [Wilderness News, historic lodges I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/); [II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
- Basswood counts: "16 resorts on Basswood Lake alone" by the mid-1940s ([Bill Rom article](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/)); "more than 20 resorts" by the late 1950s ([lodges II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/))
- Basswood Beach (Bill and Lillian Wenstrom, 1932–1967) charged $50 a week for two. Getting there meant a launch across Fall Lake, a bus over the Four Mile Portage, and another launch, "a 13-mile journey" taking 2–3 hours — [Wilderness News, Basswood Beach](https://queticosuperior.org/basswood-beach-resort-canoe-trips/)
- Thye-Blatnik (1948) provided $500,000 for buyouts plus in-lieu tax payments to Cook, Lake and St. Louis counties. It was extended in 1956 and 1961. The Izaak Walton League funded 14 strategic purchases — [Wikipedia Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter); [lodges II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
- EO 10092, verbatim (17 December 1949): "airspace below the altitude of 4,000 feet above sea level" over the Superior, Little Indian Sioux and Caribou Roadless Areas. It took effect "After January 1, 1951", with flights to private land allowed "until January 1, 1952". Exceptions covered emergencies, safety, official business and rescue — [Perko v. United States, 204 F.2d 446 (8th Cir. 1953)](https://static.case.law/f2d/204/cases/0446-01.json)
- The court case: "Defendants Perko, Skala and Zupancich operate resorts on certain border lakes", and West "is in the commercial aviation business at Ely". Judge Nordbye (26 September 1952) granted a permanent injunction with a 60-day stay. The 8th Circuit affirmed on 25 May 1953 — [US v. Perko, 108 F. Supp. 315](https://static.case.law/f-supp/108/cases/0315-01.json); [204 F.2d 446](https://static.case.law/f2d/204/cases/0446-01.json)
- Judge Nordbye's words: "From the days of the voyageurs, it has been a canoe country". Absent planes, resort owners' only access was "by canoe or boat in the summertime and by foot or snowshoe" in winter — [US v. Perko](https://static.case.law/f-supp/108/cases/0315-01.json)
- In 1952 "two pilots still landing their float planes are caught and their planes confiscated", ending flights to Isle of Pines — [Wikipedia Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter). Wilderness News: "fly-ins continued for four years as the constitutionality of the air bans was challenged". It also says the ban was "enforced by U.S. Marshalls" — [Bill Rom article](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/); [lodges II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
- Local conflict: Bill Rom hosted airban meetings, and "an explosive was set off near the Roms' house as a scare tactic" — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/); [Wikipedia CCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canoe_Country_Outfitters)
- Remote resorts after the ban: "Former residents recall hauling cabins out onto the ice and burning them down on cold winter nights" (Crooked Lake and other remote lakes). Over 40 resorts and 90+ private homes had been bought by the late 1960s. The stone foundation and steps at Curtain Falls remain — [Wilderness News, lodges II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/); [lodges I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/)
- Basswood Lodge's main building was bought by the government in 1964, hauled over the ice to Snowbank, bought again after 1978, and sold for $1 to Burnett County (WI) in 1985 — [lodges II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
- 1958: renamed the Boundary Waters Canoe Area, which "prevented the planning of a road" from Winton to Gunflint — [Wikipedia Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter); [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)
- The New Deal era: 8 NIRA camps and 30 CCC camps on the Superior NF; the South Kawishiwi log pavilion was built by the CCC — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)

### Inferences
- An Ely story set in 1949–53 has a ready conflict. Resort families, pilots and many townspeople whose livelihoods hung on fly-ins faced a small group of canoe outfitters and guides (Rom, Magie, Olson, Ambrose), with an explosion near one home. Town sentiment was split, and the canoe side was "not the popular opinion".

### Gaps
- **"Operation Snowbird" (1951 resort burnings): no source found.** Wikipedia, Wilderness News and the Forest Service history pages say nothing about a BWCA "Operation Snowbird"; the name only turns up for Air National Guard and Coast Guard operations. The documented facts are: cabins hauled onto the ice and burned (date unspecified), government purchase and removal, and planes confiscated in 1952. **Treat "Operation Snowbird" as unverified** until found in Searle/Proescholdt or newspapers.
- Which lakes Perko, Skala and Zupancich operated on is not stated in the court opinions (Crooked Lake / Curtain Falls is plausible but unconfirmed).
- Searle's *Saving Quetico-Superior* (1977) is on archive.org, but borrow-only (no full text).

## 6. Sigurd Olson, Bill Magie, Benny Ambrose, Dorothy Molter and early guides

### Takeaway
Sigurd Olson (1899–1982) came to Ely in 1923 and taught at the Ely high school and junior college (dean from 1936). He guided for decades, quit teaching in 1947, and led the airban push in the late 1940s. Bill Magie, a pioneer pilot and Corps of Engineers surveyor (1925–30) who later guided for Bill Rom, founded Friends of the Wilderness in 1949. Benny Ambrose, an Iowa-born trapper, prospector and guide on Ottertrack Lake (1920s to 1982), testified *for* the airban although it hurt his business. Dorothy Molter ran the Isle of Pines on Knife Lake from 1934.

### Cited Findings
- Olson: his first canoe trip came in June 1921, and the family moved to Ely in early 1923. He chaired science and became dean at Ely Junior College in 1936, resigning in 1947 to write. He guided for 30+ years and was called "the Bourgeois", the voyageurs' word for a trusted leader. Books: *The Singing Wilderness* (1956), *Listening Point* (1958). He bought Listening Point on Burntside in 1956 — [Wikipedia Olson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigurd_F._Olson); [Wikipedia Ely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ely,_Minnesota)
- In the late 1940s Oberholtzer "yielded the spotlight to Sigurd Olson", and the fight narrowed to fly-ins — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/ernest-c-oberholtzer-lifetime-advocate/)
- Magie: in 1949 he "founded the Friends of the Wilderness". He held an early pilot's license (said to be signed by Orville Wright) and was suspended for flying a Curtiss Jenny under Duluth's Aerial Bridge "on a $50 bet". From 1925, for five years, he did Army Corps of Engineers survey work in the future BWCA, "often traveled alone by dog sled" in winter. He later guided out of CCO's Moose Lake base and retired in 1979 at 77 — [Wikipedia CCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canoe_Country_Outfitters)
- Rom: started Canoe Country Outfitters on Sheridan Street, Ely, in 1946 with 10 wood-canvas canoes (later 400+, "Canoe King of Ely"). As a student he built the Kekekabic Trail and staffed the Kekekabic fire tower — [Wilderness News](https://queticosuperior.org/bill-rom-canoe-king-ely/)
- Ambrose: born in Iowa, he came up the Gunflint Trail in the 1920s and homesteaded on Ottertrack Lake, becoming one of the best fishing guides. He "testified in support of the initial creation of the no-fly zone in the late 1940s" despite the cost to his business, because "Canoeists got to have an area of their own" — [Wilderness News, Magical History Tour (quoting Minnesota Environmental Partnership)](https://queticosuperior.org/magical-history-tour-through-the-bwca/)
- Ambrose settled on Ottertrack in 1920 per one article ("1920s" per another) and died in 1982. His birchbark canoe is at the Chik-Wauk Museum — [Wilderness News, PMAs](https://queticosuperior.org/exploring-the-primitive-management-areas-of-the-bwcaw/); [Chik-Wauk article](https://queticosuperior.org/museum-on-gunflint-trail-opens-for-summer-with-new-facilities-and-exhibits/)
- Molter: born May 6, 1907, she first visited the Isle of Pines (Knife Lake) in 1930 and lived there from 1934. She inherited it when Bill Berglund died in 1948. After the airban she brought root-beer supplies in by boat. Condemnation was completed in 1964 but she was allowed to stay; she died in 1986 as the last resident — [Wikipedia Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)
- Other guides and characters:
  - "Big Bill" Wenstrom, an ex-Forest Service man, was the last private landowner to leave when the area became wilderness ([old photos](https://queticosuperior.org/old-photos-provide-peek-history/))
  - Count Rovigno was a 1922 forest guard who said "If you want to see how small you really are, go into the woods" (Minneapolis Journal, 1922; [USFS archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049850))
  - Forest Guard Larry Stotz sank a canoe to protect it from bears; a bear punched six holes in it ([USFS archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049850))
- 1916: Forest Service practice was that a two-man, two-week pack weighed 70–90 lb, and a new canoe 75 lb "and increased as it was repaired" — [USFS (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/2022/https://www.fs.usda.gov/detail/superior/learning/history-culture/?cid=fsm91_049843)

### Inferences
- Olson, Magie, Rom and Ambrose make a credible 1949 "canoe faction" around Ely and the Gunflint. Resort families like Perko, Skala and Zupancich and bush pilots like West form the opposing side, so an NPC set in 1950 could be drawn from either side.

### Gaps
- Bill Magie's birth year and death date are not confirmed (retired at 77 in 1979 implies born about 1902).
- No primary text of Olson's 1940s airban speeches was found.
