## 1. 1964 Wilderness Act exception, Selke Committee, Freeman Directives, logging lawsuits, snowmobiles

### Takeaway
The 1964 Wilderness Act made the BWCA an "instant wilderness". It was the only area named in the Act with special exceptions, and it left logging and motors to the Secretary of Agriculture. The Selke Committee (1964) and the Freeman Directives (1965) set no-cut zones and motor zones, which the Forest Service then weakened (snowmobiles were allowed on motor routes). The MPIRG lawsuits (1972–76) stopped logging in virgin forest until the Eighth Circuit reversed that ruling in 1976. The stalemate pushed both sides toward Congress.

### Cited Findings
- **Background buyouts before 1964.** The Thye-Blatnik Act (1948) authorized buying the remaining private homes and resorts. Truman's Executive Order 10092 (1949) banned flights below 4,000 ft. The area was named the Boundary Waters Canoe Area in 1958. — [Wikipedia: BWCAW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness)
- Thye-Blatnik provided $500,000 to buy out resorts and private land, plus in-lieu-of-tax payments to Cook, Lake and St. Louis counties. — [Wikipedia: Dorothy Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)
- Thye-Blatnik was extended and funded again in 1956 and 1961. The Izaak Walton League set up private endowment funds to buy 14 strategic properties. U.S. Marshals enforced the 1949 air ban. — [Quetico Superior Wilderness News, "Historic Lodges Part II" (2004)](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
- In 1952 two pilots who were still landing float planes were caught and their planes confiscated, which effectively ended flights to Molter's Isle of Pines. — [Wikipedia: Dorothy Molter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)
- **Origins of the 1964 Act.** Humphrey introduced the first wilderness bill in 1956. After "eight years and 66 rewrites", the BWCA was the only specific wilderness named, as an "instant wilderness" that allowed some logging and motors to continue. — [MinnPost (Bierschbach, 2015)](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
  - Pioneer Press gives "Eight years, 19 hearings and dozens of rewrites". It says the compromise allowed logging, motorboats and motorized portages, "primarily to give anglers quick access to Basswood Lake". — [Pioneer Press (2014)](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Congress passed the Wilderness Act on September 3, 1964. The BWCA was the only area with such major management exceptions. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)
- **The Selke Committee.** In spring 1964 a Twin Cities group, the Conservation Affiliates, asked Agriculture Secretary Orville Freeman to stop logging and motor use. On May 21, 1964, Freeman appointed a review committee chaired by George A. Selke. Its members were Wayne Olson (state conservation commissioner), Rollie Johnson (WCCO-TV news director) and David J. Winton (chairman of Winton Lumber Co.). It reported on December 15, 1964. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)
- **Heinselman's first fight (1964).** U.S. Forest Service forester Miron "Bud" Heinselman broke with his profession by calling for an end to logging. He estimated the area held at least 400,000 acres of virgin forest, contradicting Dean Frank Kaufert of the University of Minnesota School of Forestry. — [Proescholdt, "First Fight," *Minnesota History* 64:2 (2014)](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/64/v64i02p070-084.pdf)
- Heinselman and Kaufert debated at a closed Selke Committee session on September 17, 1964. Afterwards the Forest Service barred Heinselman from any public role on BWCA issues, even on his own time. — [Proescholdt 2014](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/64/v64i02p070-084.pdf); [Wikipedia: Miron Heinselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Lee_Heinselman)
- Heinselman's letter to Sigurd Olson asked for a policy affirming that "the American people mean to keep this unique area a Wilderness!" — [Proescholdt 2014](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/64/v64i02p070-084.pdf)
- The Selke report recommended prohibiting snowmobiles and houseboats and closing the truck portages at Prairie Portage and Four Mile Portage, but it kept logging outside the no-cut zones. It proposed Large Motor, Small Motor and No-Motor zones. — [Proescholdt 2014](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/64/v64i02p070-084.pdf)
- **The Freeman Directives** came in January 1965, according to Proescholdt. Freeman then deferred to the Forest Service, which insisted that snowmobiles be allowed on the same routes as motorboats. The draft regulations of June 1965 and the final ones of December 1965 weakened the directives further. — [Proescholdt 2014](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/64/v64i02p070-084.pdf)
  - Wikipedia dates the Freeman Directive December 15, 1965. That is probably the date of the final regulations. It says the directive added 150,000 acres of no-cut immediately and 100,000 more in 1975, for 612,000 acres of no-cut in total. Motorboats stayed on over half the water area, and snowmobiles were allowed on designated routes. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act). **The two sources conflict on the date.**
- The Freeman Directive created an Interior Zone with no commercial logging and a Portal Zone where logging was allowed except within 400 ft of canoe waters and portages. About 200,000 acres of virgin forest were left in the Portal Zone. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)
- **Snowmobiles.** In 1964 the Forest Service ruled snowmobiles to be "winter motorboats", which made the Voyageur's Highway a major snowmobile route. Winter traffic past Benny Ambrose's cabin soon exceeded the summer canoe traffic. — [*Minnesota History* 54:3 (1994), "Benny Ambrose"](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/54/v54i03p124-137.pdf)
- **The logging lawsuits.** MPIRG filed *MPIRG v. Butz* on November 24, 1972. On April 16, 1973, Judge Miles Lord enjoined seven timber contracts in virgin forest pending an environmental impact statement (EIS). After the EIS came out in August 1974, MPIRG and the Sierra Club sued again. Trial began November 4, 1974, and Lord ruled on August 13, 1975 that cutting virgin forest violated the Wilderness Act. The Eighth Circuit reversed him on August 30, 1976, and the injunctions were lifted by December 1976. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)

### Inferences
- From 1964 to 1978 the area was "wilderness" in name, but in practice truck portages, motor routes, snowmobile trails and logging roads remained. That is why people on both sides felt cheated. Locals felt something promised in 1964 was being taken away. Advocates felt the "wilderness" was not one.
- George Selke was Freeman's former commissioner of conservation (Freeman was governor 1955–61). This suggests the committee was chosen to be palatable to both sides; it included a lumber executive.

### Gaps
- Exact text of the 1964 Act's BWCA clause (Section 4(d)(5)) was not fetched.
- No primary account found of a specific 1960s–70s snowmobile confrontation, beyond the 1978 protests below.

## 2. The 1978 BWCA Wilderness Act: bills, hearings, effigies, groups, provisions, quotes

### Takeaway
Rep. Jim Oberstar (8th District, which includes Ely) proposed splitting the area into a smaller wilderness and a motor/logging recreation area. Wilderness advocates led by Bud Heinselman's Friends of the Boundary Waters Wilderness (founded May 1976) backed Rep. Don Fraser's full-wilderness bill. The July 1977 field hearings, in St. Paul on July 7 and in Ely on July 8, became the emotional peak: effigies of Sigurd Olson and Heinselman hung from a logging truck, and Olson was booed for minutes. A Burton–Vento bill and then the Dayton–Walls negotiation produced the final law, which Carter signed on October 21, 1978. It ended logging, phased out snowmobiles, cut motorboats to about a quarter of the water, created a Mining Protection Area and added land.

### Cited Findings
**The bills**
- **Oberstar's bill.** On October 24, 1975, Oberstar introduced H.R. 10247: about 625,000 acres of full wilderness and 527,000 acres as a National Recreation Area open to logging and motors. Friends of the Boundary Waters, with Kevin Proescholdt among the leaders, was "among the largest" opposition groups. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)
  - MinnPost instead gives 1976, a 600,000-acre wilderness and a nearly 400,000-acre recreation area. — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/). **The sources conflict on the date and acreage.**
- Oberstar (1977) wanted "more broad-based recreational use of the BWCA, plus some very limited commercial timber harvesting in the second growth areas". — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act, citing MPR 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)
- Oberstar called the post-1964 regime "semi-wilderness, semi-recreational use with escape clauses". — [MPR News (2017)](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/01/23/how-ely-has-handled-economic-environmental-concerns)
- **Fraser's bill.** Don Fraser (DFL-Minneapolis, 5th District) introduced a bill making the whole BWCA wilderness and ending logging, motors and mining. Wikipedia numbers it H.R. 14576. Heinselman's September 1977 testimony refers to "H.R. 2820 – The Fraser Bill". — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act); [Wikipedia: Heinselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Lee_Heinselman). **The bill number is uncertain.** The 1977 reintroduction in the new Congress probably got the new number.
- MinnPost says environmentalists "enlisted" Fraser, and that the move "pitted Democrats against Democrats". — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)

**The groups**
- **Friends of the Boundary Waters Wilderness.** Founded in May 1976 by Bud and Fran Heinselman, Chuck Dayton and others, with Heinselman as unpaid chair. Bud and Fran lived for months at a time in Washington, D.C. at their own expense; Heinselman carried "tubes stuffed with maps" through the Capitol. — [Chuck Dayton, Friends of the BW (2020)](https://www.friends-bwca.org/blog/bud-heinselman/); [Wikipedia: Heinselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Lee_Heinselman)
- Heinselman took early retirement from the Forest Service in 1974 to campaign. He mapped the 20-plus additions himself, died February 28, 1993, and co-wrote *Troubled Waters* (1995). — [Wikipedia: Heinselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Lee_Heinselman)
- **On the motor-access side:** Nancy McReady ran "Conservationists with Common Sense" for about 25 years (as of 2014). Its founding date is not given; it may postdate 1978. — [Pioneer Press (2014)](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Ely City Attorney Ron Walls negotiated the final deal for the local side. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)

**The mood in Ely, 1975–78**
- Sports Illustrated (1977), as quoted by MinnPost: locals were "aroused over attempts by 'outside agitators'". Bumper stickers read "SIERRA CLUB KISS MY AXE". A Forest Service building was burned, tires of pro-wilderness people were slashed, and a pro-Fraser outfitter's store window was broken "a couple of times". — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
- **Bill Rom.** Rom was the outfitter known as Ely's "Canoe King" and a wilderness supporter. In 1975 protesters blocked access to his business on the two busiest weekends of summer, and he sold it that year after a 30-year run. His daughter Becky Rom: "He was being picketed by people he had lived with his whole life." — [Pioneer Press (2014)](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- **Joe Baltich** was 17 in 1978 and later owned Northwind Lodge. He recalls "long blockades of cars preventing canoeists from entering", and says: "I spent the whole summer being scared." — [Pioneer Press (2014)](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Baltich, on how locals felt: "you're a little itty, bitty group of people up against this great big environmental movement." — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- In April 1978 protesters marched up Fifth Avenue West in Duluth to the Federal Building against further restrictions on motorboats and snowmobiles (Duluth News Tribune photo). — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
- Sigurd Olson had been at odds with Ely for decades. Chuck Wick, of the Listening Point Foundation, says that at outfitters' meetings in the 1920s–30s, Olson's wife and his partner Pete Peterson's wife "wondered whether they'd come back alive". In the 1940s Olson lobbied for the float-plane ban and the federal buyouts. — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)

**The 1977 field hearings**
- **St. Paul, July 7, 1977**, in a packed State Capitol hearing room on a day in the high 80s. State Rep. Willard Munger (DFL-Duluth): "national interests must supersede local concerns." — [MPR News (2017)](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/01/23/how-ely-has-handled-economic-environmental-concerns)
- **Ely, July 8, 1977.** More than 1,000 people packed the auditorium. A dummy hung by its neck from the boom of a logging truck, with Olson's and Heinselman's names pinned to its shirt. — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
  - MPR says logging trucks lined the streets and effigies of both men hung from a truck. — [MPR 2017](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/01/23/how-ely-has-handled-economic-environmental-concerns)
  - The Heinselman article places the effigies outside the Ely auditorium. — [Wikipedia: Heinselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Lee_Heinselman)
  - **Fraser effigy:** no source found that says Fraser was hung in effigy in Ely. The sources name only Olson and Heinselman.
- When Rep. Bruce Vento (chairing) called Olson, the hall erupted in boos for minutes. Vento asked an aide whether there was a way out in case the crowd turned into a mob. — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
- Proescholdt, who was there as a volunteer: "Vento was pounding and pounding the gavel trying to regain order." — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- **What Olson said once the hall quieted:**
  - "Some places should be preserved from development or exploitation for they satisfy a human need for solace, belonging and perspective." — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
  - From the same testimony: had any of the past fights (roads, dams, fly-in resorts, logging, mining) "been lost there would be no wilderness in the BWCA today." — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
  - A related Olson line (Pioneer Press says it echoed his 1974 speech to the Northern Environmental Council): "Wilderness has no price." — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Olson was then 78. He died in January 1982. The last words in his typewriter were "A new adventure is coming up, and I'm sure it will be a good one." — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Benny Ambrose was interviewed on Ottertrack Lake on September 1, 1977, during the fight; the tape is at the Forest Service district office in Ely. — [*Minnesota History* 54:3](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/54/v54i03p124-137.pdf)

**The compromise and the law**
- In March 1978 Reps. Phillip Burton (California, subcommittee chair) and Bruce Vento (Minnesota) put forward a new bill ending logging. It stalled until the Dayton–Walls agreement between Ely City Attorney Ron Walls and environmental attorney Chuck Dayton. In the last hours of the 95th Congress, on October 15, 1978, both chambers passed the revised H.R. 12250. Carter signed it on October 21, 1978 as Pub. L. 95-495 (92 Stat. 1649), effective October 1, 1979. The House had passed its version on June 5, 1978. — [Wikipedia: BWCA Wilderness Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act)
- **Provisions**, all from the [Wikipedia article on the Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_Waters_Canoe_Area_Wilderness_Act) unless noted:
  - **Name and size.** The area was renamed BWCA Wilderness. More than 50,000 acres were added, for 1,098,057 acres in total.
  - **Motors.** Motorboats were limited to about 24% of the water surface. Heinselman's article says motor water fell from 62% to about 21%, and MinnPost says "one-fourth". **The sources disagree on the exact share.**
  - **25 hp lakes:** Trout, Fall, Moose, Newfound, Sucker, Newton, South Farm, East Bearskin, Snowbank, Saganaga east of American Point, and Basswood (except northwest of Washington Island and north of Jackfish Bay). Towboats were exempt on Moose, Newfound, Sucker and Saganaga until January 1, 1984.
  - **10 hp lakes:** Sea Gull east of Threemile Island, Clearwater, North and South Fowl, Island River east of Isabella, Alder, and Canoe.
  - **Phase-outs:** Basswood River to Crooked Lake, and Carp Lake, Knife River and Knife Lake, at 10 hp until January 1, 1984. Brule until 1994. Sea Gull west of Threemile until 1999.
  - **No hp limit:** Little Vermilion, Loon River, Loon Lake, and Lac La Croix south of Snow Bay and east of Wilkins Bay.
  - **Snowmobiles** were banned except on the Crane Lake–Little Vermilion portage to Canada and the Sea Gull River–Saganaga route to Canada. Moose–Ensign–Vera–Knife and East Bearskin–Alder–Canoe–Pine routes stayed open until January 1, 1984, for machines under 40 inches wide.
  - **Logging.** All timber contracts were to be ended within one year, virgin-forest cutting immediately, with compensation paid.
  - **Mining.** The Secretary may acquire mineral rights in the wilderness and in a 222,000-acre Mining Protection Area along three road corridors.
  - **Quotas.** Motor quotas are capped at the 1976–78 average use. Cabin owners, resorts and their guests are exempt on their own lakes.
  - **Court challenges.** Minnesota challenged the Act in court from 1979 to 1982 and lost in the Eighth Circuit. The Supreme Court declined review in 1982.
- The 1978 law repealed the 1964 BWCA logging and motor clause, "the only time that the landmark 1964 law has been directly amended". — [Wikipedia: Heinselman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miron_Lee_Heinselman)
- Motor-side advocate Nancy McReady (2014): motors remain on 18 large lakes "out of more than 1,100", and "That's part of what was promised". — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)

**Political fallout**
- **Fraser.** Fraser won the DFL endorsement for Humphrey's old Senate seat. Bob Short beat him in the September 12, 1978 primary by 3,451 votes, running ads in the 8th District that cast Fraser as a Twin Cities elitist. Short lost to David Durenberger in November. — [MNopedia "Minnesota Massacre"](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minnesota-massacre-1978-election); [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
- Fraser aide Iric Nathanson: "People felt they should be able to run their motorboats where they wanted to". — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
- **Wendell Anderson** had resigned as governor in 1976 so that Rudy Perpich would appoint him to Mondale's Senate seat. In 1978 Rudy Boschwitz beat him by more than 250,000 votes, and he carried only three counties (all 87 in 1974). MNopedia attributes the loss mainly to the "self-appointment", not to the BWCA. — [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minnesota-massacre-1978-election)

### Inferences
- The Ely hearing (logging trucks, the dummy on the boom, minutes of booing, Vento looking for an exit) is the most vivid, well-sourced set piece of the whole fight. It is suitable for a remembered scene told by an old outfitter or a former logger's child.
- The final deal was struck between a local lawyer (Walls) and a metro lawyer (Dayton). That pairing could frame a story of reconciliation.

### Gaps
- **Wendell Anderson's specific role in the BWCA bill.** Anderson is often said to have brokered the Senate compromise, but I found no source for this in this session. Treat it as unverified. MNopedia does not tie his defeat to the BWCA.
- Exact names of the 1977–78 local opposition groups are unconfirmed. Neither "Boundary Waters Conservation Alliance" nor other "Citizens for…" names turned up, and searches were rate-limited.
- No source found for an effigy of Fraser in Ely.
- Wilderness lakes now named for the advocates (e.g., a Quetico lake named for Heinselman in 2019): the Friends' 2020 blog post mentions the naming, but the lake name was not captured.

## 3. Resort buyouts: which resorts, which families, what happened to the buildings

### Takeaway
By the early 1940s there were more than 40 resorts inside today's BWCAW, and more than 20 of them were on Basswood alone. The 1949 air ban killed the fly-in camps (Crooked Lake, Curtain Falls). Thye-Blatnik money (1948, 1956, 1961) and the 1964 Act bought out the rest: more than 40 resorts and more than 90 private homes by the late 1960s. Buildings were burned on the ice, dismantled and resold, or moved to camps outside. Today stone foundations, dock footings, stone steps, rock landing walls and feral garden plants remain. Some owners stayed bitter; others later accepted the result.

### Cited Findings
- Before the buyouts there were more than 40 resorts in today's BWCAW, more than 20 on Basswood, and others on Kekekabic, Crooked, Seagull, Lac La Croix and Knife. They employed over 300 people a year. In the late 1940s up to 38–40 seaplane flights a day flew from Ely, the largest freshwater seaplane base in the continental U.S. One promoter advertised a "non-stop flight from Florida to Crooked Lake". — [Quetico Superior Wilderness News, Part I (2003)](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/); [Timberjay (2012)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)
- **Named resorts on Basswood:** Basswood Lodge & Wilderness Outfitters (founded 1921 as a fishing camp, room for 100+ guests, two seaplane flights a day to Ely), Pipestone Falls Lodge, Skidway Borderline Lodge, Pinecliff Lodge, Johnson Bros. Fishing Camp, Evergreen Lodge, and Peterson Fishing Camp. — [QS Wilderness News, Part II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/); [Part I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/)
- **Crooked Lake:** the Curtain Falls Fishing Camp, reached only by seaplane, "rumored" to have had a slot machine and a popular bar. — [QS Wilderness News, Part II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
- Perko's Crooked Lake Resort: Paul Harvey called it a "Waldorf in the Wilderness". — [Timberjay (2012)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)
- **Basswood Lodge staff:** Joe Kerntz was a principal, and Jim and Mary Kerntz managed it. **1964 was its last season.** — [Timberjay (2012)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)
  - Mary Ann Shober, who worked the kitchen that last summer: "People I worked with are my best friends today." — same source
- Former Basswood Lodge guide Karl Rukavina, at 80: "There isn't a day that goes by when I don't think about my time as a guide at Basswood Lodge." — [Timberjay (2012)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)
  - On the fishing then: catching at least 100 walleyes a day per party "was not uncommon". — same source
  - On the change: canoeists' purchases in town "pale in comparison" to what the resort fishermen bought. — same source
- **Pete's Cabin Boats (Pete's Houseboats), Basswood, 1933–1970.** A floating resort owned by Jacob "Jake" Pete and later his sons Jim and Leonard. The fleet: Selma A., Jacob L., Gloria Mae, Leonard G., Russell Lee, Gail Adena and Capri III, plus the launch Marnice on Fall Lake and a truck called the "Blue Goose" on Four Mile Portage. Guests included actress Joan Davis and cartoonist Al Capp. Federal buyouts came in the 1960s. The Jacob L. and Gail Adena were sold and moved to Rainy Lake, still in use in 2004. — [Timberjay (David Kess, 2004)](https://timberjay.com/stories/it-was-a-piece-of-basswood-history,1041)
- **What happened to the buildings:**
  - Basswood Lodge's main log building was bought by the government in 1964, dismantled, sold to a local entrepreneur and hauled across the ice to become Snowbank Lodge. In 1978 part of Snowbank Lake went into the BWCAW, and the government bought the same building again and planned to destroy it. In 1985 Burnett County, Wisconsin bought it for $1.00. It is now the visitor center at Forts Folle Avoine Historical Park, Danbury, WI. — [QS Wilderness News, Part II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
  - Parts of Pinecliff and Evergreen lodges were bought by Wilderness Canoe Base (Plymouth Youth Center) and moved to the Seagull chain. — same source
  - Crooked Lake and other remote resorts, cut off by the seaplane ban, were removed or destroyed. Former residents recall "hauling cabins out onto the ice and burning them down on cold winter nights." — same source
  - Wilderness Outfitters, which began at Basswood Lodge, survived in Ely. Some displaced businesses moved to Lac La Croix and Crane Lake, just outside the boundary. — same source
- **Remains today:** above Curtain Falls, "remains of a rough stone foundation, dock footings, and stone steps to a portage". — [QS Wilderness News, Part I](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters/)
- A paddler on the bwca.com forum (2012) remembers a resort just east of Curtain Falls: after its removal, "we found progressively wilder vegetables in the old garden site for several years". — [bwca.com forum "old resorts"](https://bwca.com/index.cfm?fuseaction=forum.thread&threadid=475343&confid=1&forumid=12) (anecdotal)
- Forum users report elaborate rock landing walls at Basswood campsites 1607 (near Pipestone Falls) and 1562, possibly from pre-wilderness cabins. Another user disputes their age. — [bwca.com forum "Old Basswood Resorts?" (2023)](https://bwca.com/index.cfm?fuseaction=forum.thread&confId=1&forumId=12&threadId=1331251) (anecdotal, uncertain). The Fosse family cabin on Norway Island, Basswood, is mentioned there too.
- Brule Lake had the Brule Island Camp and a camp used by the Ruan Trucking Company. Brule Island Camp moored its float planes across the lake, outside the boundary. — [bwca.com forum](https://bwca.com/index.cfm?fuseaction=forum.thread&threadid=475343&confid=1&forumid=12) (anecdotal)
- Wegen's Wilderness Camp on Newton Lake was started in 1924. Doris Wegen Patton remembers ice cream made with ice stored in sawdust, and trading with Native people arriving by birch-bark canoe. The family later built a private home on Basswood. — [QS Wilderness News, Part II](https://queticosuperior.org/historic-lodges-boundary-waters-part-ii/)
- **Bitterness and acceptance:**
  - Helen Koski's father built a fly-in lodge on Crooked Lake in the 1930s. The float-plane ban "crashed the business" and he sold it. Her view now: "I'm glad it's like it is. I'd hate to see Crooked Lake and Basswood Lake with cabin after cabin." — [Pioneer Press (2014)](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
  - The Timberjay: many resort owners "were not ready to give it up". It quotes writer Simon Bourgin: "Yet everybody wants it on their own terms." — [Timberjay (2012)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)

### Inferences
- Most resort removals predate 1978: the 1950s for fly-in camps, 1964–1970 for Basswood. The 1978 Act mainly took remaining edge properties (Snowbank) and motor routes. A story set in 1977–78 would have Basswood families already about ten to fifteen years past their loss.
- Physical traces you could put in the game: stone foundations, dock cribs, stone steps, rock landing walls, feral garden vegetables, cut-stone cairns. Lilacs and rhubarb are commonly reported at old homesteads, but no source in this session says so specifically for the BWCA.

### Gaps
- No appraisal figures or condemnation case names were found for specific resorts.
- No source found for the family names behind Pipestone Falls, Pinecliff or Curtain Falls, or for the Lac La Croix, Saganaga and Knife Lake resorts other than Isle of Pines.
- Lilacs and rhubarb at old sites are unconfirmed.

## 4. People who lived inside: Dorothy Molter and Benny Ambrose

### Takeaway
Dorothy Molter (Knife Lake, 1934–1986) and Benny Ambrose (Ottertrack Lake, about 1920s–1982) refused to sell. Their properties were condemned around 1964, with permission to stay until 1975. Public outcry, and friends in high places (Humphrey, Mondale, a Supreme Court justice), led the Forest Service to make them "volunteers in service", so they could live out their lives. After Ambrose died, rangers burned his homestead and left a rock cairn. After Molter died, volunteers hauled her cabins to Ely by dogsled and snowmobile for a museum.

### Cited Findings
**Dorothy Molter (1907–1986), "the Root Beer Lady", Isle of Pines, Knife Lake**
- **Early life.** Born May 6, 1907 in Arnold, Pennsylvania. Raised partly in a Cincinnati orphanage, then in Chicago, where she trained as a nurse.
- **Coming to Knife Lake.** First visited Bill Berglund's Isle of Pines Resort in 1930 and lived there from 1934. Berglund taught her to portage, dogsled and cut ice. She managed his diabetes. Outfitters marked the Isle of Pines on maps as a place for medical help, earning her the name "Nightingale of the Northwoods".
- **Running it alone.** Inherited the resort when Berglund died in 1948.
- **Supplies after the air ban.** After 1949, supplies came 15 miles by water and portage from Moose Lake. Locals called "Dorothy's Angels" brought her mail and supplies. Because bottled pop could no longer be packed in, she began brewing root beer, stirred with a canoe paddle.
- **Fame.** The Saturday Evening Post ran "The Loneliest Woman in America" in October 1952. She logged up to 6,000 visitors a summer.
- Sources for the above: [MNopedia (Laura Hanson)](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/molter-dorothy-1907-1986); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)
- **Daily life.** No electricity or phone. Propane for cooking, a battery 2-way radio, a wood-heated cabin, and an icehouse with hand-cut lake ice that lasted through August. Summers she lived in a layered canvas tent on a satellite island. Footbridges linked the islands, and she had a fence of broken paddles donated by visitors. Flowers grew in old fishing boats filled with hauled dirt. Her guest book gathered more than 4,000 signatures a summer, often over 100 visitors a day in July and August. After 1964 she couldn't legally sell root beer, so visitors made "donations". She rode a Polaris Mustang snowmobile in the 1970s. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter); [MNopedia image captions](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/molter-dorothy-1907-1986)
- **Legal arc.** Efforts to buy her land began in 1948. Condemnation was completed in 1964. After public outcry, including Sen. Walter Mondale, she and Ambrose were allowed to stay until 1975. In 1975 both became Forest Service "volunteers in service". The 1978 Act banned motors and snowmobiles in her area from 1984. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)
  - Northern Wilds says instead that she "was granted lifetime tenancy in 1972". — [Northern Wilds (2016)](https://northernwilds.com/the-lasting-legacy-of-the-root-beer-lady/). **The sources conflict on 1972 vs 1975.**
- **Death and museum.** She died December 18, 1986 at 79, the last full-time resident. In 1987 volunteers dismantled the homestead, moved it to Ely by dogsled and snowmobile, and restored two cabins (the Winter Cabin and the Point Cabin) as the Dorothy Molter Museum, 2002 E Sheridan St, Ely. — [Northern Wilds](https://northernwilds.com/the-lasting-legacy-of-the-root-beer-lady/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorothy_Molter)

**Benny Ambrose (c. 1896–1982), Ottertrack Lake**
- **Early life.** Born about 1896; the dates he gave ranged from 1894 to 1899. Ran away from an Iowa farm near Amana at 14, explaining: "married a lady that I called the devil's grandmother". Served in the Rainbow Division in WWI. An Ojibwe army friend from Grand Portage drew him to northeastern Minnesota to prospect, and he arrived at Hovland in 1919. — [*Minnesota History* 54:3 (Fall 1994)](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/54/v54i03p124-137.pdf)
- **The homestead.** He bought a 19-acre point on Ottertrack from Lloyd K. Johnson of Duluth with a handshake, because it was "the prettiest spot I ever saw". He made a garden from beaver-pond muck and sacks of Iowa soil and manure. He trapped, guided and prospected, finding gold, silver, nickel, cobalt and other deposits, none in paying quantity. His wife Val left with their two daughters (Bonnie and Holly), who spent summers with him. He brought Molter vegetables and helped her start a garden. — same source; [Boundary Waters Blog (Sue Prom, 2018)](https://www.boundarywatersblog.com/benny-ambrose-of-ottertrack-lake/)
- **Reputation.** Justine Kerfoot wrote of his pride in "being able to carry the heaviest load". — [*Minnesota History* 54:3](https://storage.googleapis.com/mnhs-org-support/mn_history_articles/54/v54i03p124-137.pdf)
- **Fight with the government.** In 1964 the government barred storing boats in the wilderness. He wrote to a fishing client who was a Supreme Court justice and got an exception. Negotiations through the 1950s–60s turned into condemnation threats. When officials came to serve condemnation papers, he turned them over and "looked out the window thoughtfully". Lake County transferred title to the USFS in April 1964 for an estimated $35,000–$50,000 (the price was never officially found), with the right to stay until 1975. — same source
- **Allowed to stay.** In 1973–74 eviction was expected. Instead Superior National Forest Supervisor Harold Anderson made him a wilderness volunteer, saying: "I'll be damned if I could be a part of throwing them out." — same source
- **Later years.** Four alarm clocks set to different times. A poster: "Worry is like a rocking chair". — same source
- **Death.** Found dead on about August 27, 1982 beside the burned-out remains of his canvas cooking shelter. His daughters scattered his ashes on the lake. At the memorial a canoe paddle, an axe, a beaver trap and an old felt hat lay on the table.
- **The homestead razed.** Days later a Forest Service crew burned and removed the buildings. Only rock foundations and hewn stone steps remain, along with a cone-shaped rock cairn the rangers built. A plaque with his etched portrait, by Canadian game warden John Beauchard, stands on the Canadian shore. — same source

### Inferences
- The two arcs make good story spines: refusal, then condemnation, then public outcry, then being reinvented as a "volunteer", then death and erasure (Ambrose) or relocation (Molter).
- A player at Ottertrack might find the stone steps and cairn. On Knife Lake there are only empty islands, because the cabins are in Ely.

### Gaps
- Other life-estate holders, if any, were not found.
- Molter's own direct quotes were not captured. The museum's "Dorothy's story" page returned 404.

## 5. Towns' economic and emotional fallout: Ely after the Pioneer Mine, outsiders, lasting bitterness

### Takeaway
Ely's last iron mine, the Pioneer, closed in April 1967; many residents remember it as "the day the town died". That same decade the resorts were bought out, and in the next one motors and snowmobiles were restricted. Locals experienced the 1978 Act as metro and national "outsiders" closing their lakes. Bitterness has softened (Baltich, Koski, Rom), but complaints about permits and Forest Service control persist. The copper-nickel mining fight has revived the same divide.

### Cited Findings
- The Soudan Mine closed December 15, 1962, and about 200 Soudan miners moved to the Pioneer. It was "wet, muddy, and dangerous". Some families packed lunch pails for three generations. — [Timberjay (Mike Hillman, 2007)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/forty-years-ago-the-pioneer-mine-shut-down,3199)
- Hillman puts the closing on April 1, 1967. The Pioneer was the last underground mine on the Vermilion Range. It closed because the steel industry's shift from open-hearth to blast furnaces made Vermilion ore unnecessary, not because the ore ran out. — [Timberjay (Mike Hillman, 2007)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/forty-years-ago-the-pioneer-mine-shut-down,3199)
- Many Ely residents remember the Pioneer's last shift (April 1967) as "the day the town died". Some say no "bunch of canoeists" will ever save the town. — [Timberjay (2017)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/fifty-years-gone-but-not-forgotten,13567)
- In a 1955 Pioneer cave-in, miner Bill "Willie" Erzar escaped a mudslide, and his partner Joe Glinsek was buried. — same source
- Ely mayor Chuck Novak (2017): "everybody on the outside's trying to tell you how the heck to run your life." — [MPR News (2017)](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/01/23/how-ely-has-handled-economic-environmental-concerns)
- Locals complain that permits favor out-of-town guests. — [MPR News (2017)](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/01/23/how-ely-has-handled-economic-environmental-concerns)
- Retired miner Paul Maki applied for 29 motor day-use permits in 2014 and got 8. — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Iric Nathanson describes the class framing: Iron Range workers "getting dirty" against "'elitists' down in the cities" in canoes. — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
- Becky Rom (2014): the mining debate has brought up "bitter memories of what happened in the '50s, '60s and '70s." — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Baltich and Rom say the anger "has slowly subsided". McReady does not want to reopen old battles, but misses winter snowmobiling and ice fishing in the BWCA as "a big part of our culture here". — [Pioneer Press](https://www.twincities.com/2014/09/12/remembering-the-boundary-waters-battles-that-led-to-its-preservation/)
- Gov. Mark Dayton (in 2015) compared the PolyMet permit to the BWCA fight, calling it "the most momentous, difficult and controversial decision". He was just starting his political career during the 1970s fight. — [MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/politics-policy/2015/11/how-specter-decades-long-fight-over-bwca-hangs-over-polymet/)
- **Tourism vs resource economy:** resort guests used to buy everything in town and resort owners wintered in Ely and Winton. Now many owners leave for the winter, and canoeists spend less. — [Timberjay (2012)](https://www.timberjay.com/stories/the-days-of-wilderness-resorts,10191)

### Inferences
- In about 1977–78 Ely had lost its mines (1967), its interior resorts (by about 1970) and its float-plane trade (1949–52). It was now being told it would lose motor routes and snowmobiling. That cumulative loss, not the 1978 Act alone, best explains the effigy-level anger.
- Even advocates such as Bill Rom were locals, so the conflict split neighbors and not only "Ely vs. the Cities". This suits a story about people on both sides of one town.
