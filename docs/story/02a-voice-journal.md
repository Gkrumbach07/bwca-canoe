# Voice guide: Walt's notebook

This guide is for anyone writing pages of Walt's notebook. Walt was an old Finnish-American guide from Ely, and the notebook
is his private record of about ten years of solo trips. It sits under `00-owner-decisions.md`. Where the two disagree,
the canon wins. The story bible decides the years, the lakes and what each clue is. This file only decides how the
pages sound.

Where the text appears in the game:
- **The sleep cutscene, first night.** The player reads by headlamp. Pages turn: years of calm solo trips, then the
  last page. The early pages need to be dull in a good way, so the last page lands.
- **The book's journal tab.** Pages are added as the player finds or understands them. Each page has to make sense on
  its own, because the player reads them out of order.
- **Clue pages.** These are ordinary entries that only become clues once something someone said, or something on the
  map, unlocks them. Each one has to still read as a fishing trip.

## Part 1. How real trippers write about solo trips

These notes come from about 25 bwca.com trip reports (more than half of them solo, two by trippers in their sixties to
eighties, and one typed from a 1992 paper notebook) and 8 forum threads. Everything below is paraphrase. The source list
is at the end.

### Rhythm

- **Entries are built around the day, not around feelings.** A typical day goes: wake time, the weather, breakfast, the
  time they got on the water, the lakes and portages in order, where they camped, supper, and the time they went to bed.
  Feelings come last, in a sentence or two, if at all.
- **The route gets a line of its own.** Many reports close a day with just the chain of lakes, separated by commas,
  sometimes after a tilde. The paper-notebook writer started each day with a count line: four lakes, three portages.
- **Clock times are everywhere.** People record when they were on the water, when they reached the site and when the
  storm hit. They're proud of an early start and they note how long a portage took ("2:15, not bad for my age").
- **The tense wanders.** People drift between past and present, especially in notes written at camp. The 1992 writer
  apologised for this and kept it anyway.
- **Later comments sit in brackets.** The person who typed up the 1992 notes added remarks from decades later in
  square brackets, like "I have no idea why." Remarks written over the top of old entries are a real habit, and Walt
  uses it (see Part 3).

### What they record

- **Wind first, always.** Wind gets a direction and a speed, often in mph, and it gets mentioned again when it changes.
  Solo writers treat wind as the thing that decides the day: a run of gusty days spoils the fishing, and wind is why
  they stay put. Windbound days have their own small genre of reading, napping, sitting on a rock and not thinking.
- **Portages are lengths in rods.** "The 173 rod", "a short 57 rods" and "200-rod single portage" all appear. The
  details people add are the climb, mud, slick rock, downed trees ("over, under, around"), the landing, and whether
  they single or double portaged. One solo writer gave each tree across a bad trail a name.
- **Landings and sites get graded.** People note ledgerock, sand, a boulder field or muck. They rate the tent pad, the
  view, shelter from wind, whether there's a spot for the tarp, the latrine, and the mess earlier campers left behind.
  Sites are named by where they are ("the point", "the NW site", "the island site"), sometimes by Forest Service number.
- **Water level and temperature.** People call the water low or high for the time of year, describe rocks near the
  surface, and note a frozen water bottle or ice on the pot.
- **Fish are logged with lure, length and method.** For example: a red spoon, small pike, longest 16 inches; walleye
  19 to 21 inches on a slow retrieve; a laker on the first drift. People tell you when the fish weren't biting.
- **Wildlife comes as sightings.** Loons, eagles, otters, a moose standing in the river, a lynx swimming, a rabbit
  crashing through camp, wolves heard between thunderclaps. A wolf seen on a portage gets a calm step-by-step account:
  stood still, made noise, waited five minutes.
- **Food and coffee are a ritual.** Breakfasts are listed item by item. Coffee is the first act of the day and a small
  luxury, and one writer called afternoon coffee and chocolate a form of worship. The fire, tea and the evening drink
  bracket the day.
- **Other people get counted.** People note the last party they saw, who was at the site they wanted, and a
  motorboat far off. Solitude is measured in days without seeing anyone. Strangers' canoes make for idle puzzles:
  where did they come in, and where are they headed?
- **Gear breaks and gets fixed.** One writer's yoke broke from dry rot, so they braced it with dead poles and carried
  on.

### How they write fear, solitude, grief and humour

- **Fear is written flatly, in order, as actions.** The longest solo account read was a swamping in spring flood: an
  hour in cold water, a pack let go, swept through rapids, pinned by the canoe, vomiting, a tarp as a blanket, then
  drawing a map from memory on a bandage wrapper. The words for feelings are plain ("I was hypothermic") and the
  verbs carry the fear. A storm at night is told the same way: the corner tie broke, they held the tarp up for 15
  minutes, everything got soaked.
- **Solitude makes people talk to things.** Forum soloists talk to the notebook, to trees and to chipmunks, and they
  remember to keep it quiet because sound carries over water. They name the mind's tricks: noises in dripping mist the
  first night, what-am-I-doing-here. Older soloists say these fade with age, and that the question that remains is
  "why am I trying to kill myself", which they answer with a nap.
- **Grief is said once, at the end, sideways.** The best example was a fishing report that only in its last day
  mentions the dog watching the sunrise, and only then says it was his last one. One sentence, and then back to the
  fish. An 83-year-old writer turned back at the landing, said no more overnight solos, sadly, and closed on the
  memories. No one dwells.
- **Humour is dry and turned on yourself.** People mock their own heavy canoe, their age and their knees, and their
  own bad decisions about mud. They swear mildly about deer flies and dams, they stay sarcastic about the weather
  ("couldn't be any worse, right?"), and they understate disasters ("No harm done"). Boards tease each other about
  bass.
- **Old-timers carry the history lightly.** A 1968 Scout crew's journal reads as dated day headers and rough-water notes. They mention it in passing: when permits started, canned food being
  banned, maps they marked by hand, a picnic table at an old site, the camp guiding of the sixties, the Grumman.

### Shorthand seen in the sources

`EP 25`, `140 rd`/`140r`, `PMA`, `LLC` (Lac La Croix), `Q` (Quetico), `FS` (Forest Service), `SNF`, `single`/`double`
(portaging), `windbound`, `layover day`, `biffy`/`thunderbox`, `the Q`, `tip-up`, `laker`, `eaters`, `smallies`, plus
compass letters for wind and sites (`NW site`, `SE 20`).

## Part 2. Who Walt is on the page

What the voice needs to know (the bible sets the facts):

- He has guided since the 1960s. He started as a swamper, worked the 1970s exploration crews with his brother, and then
  spent decades guiding and working the counter at one outfitter after another. In the notebook years he is somewhere
  between his late sixties and his mid seventies. His knees are going, and he never complains about it twice.
- He is Finnish-American Ely stock. He paces off portages in rods out of habit, drank lake water for forty years, and
  still keeps a sauna. A few Finnish words slip in when nobody is watching.
- He keeps the notebook for himself. He knows it could be found, because he has seen other men's things found, so it
  is written for a reader he doesn't trust.
- The notebook covers roughly ten years of solo trips (the bible fixes the years). It has three stretches, and the
  voice changes across them without Walt ever saying it has:
  1. **The quiet years.** Ordinary solo trips. The longest entries are here, and the dry humour. He notes the fish,
     the birds and the coffee. Now and then a line about the boys or the old days.
  2. **The checking years.** The company is back under a new name. The entries stay ordinary, but some trips go to the
     same place every year with a thin excuse (a fishing spot, a "look at the beaver"). There are short lines that only
     make sense later. He starts counting things that aren't wildlife.
  3. **The watched years.** The entries get shorter. He notes people: the same canoe twice, a man at the landing with no
     fishing gear, a truck at the put-in. He tests the people around him. Then comes the last page.

### The core of the voice

**Sparse.** Fragments are normal. Articles and subjects drop out: "Up 5:10. Fog. Coffee on the rock." Most entries run
60 to 150 words. A long entry is a sign something happened.

**He notices everything and remarks on little of it.** He records the wind veering before the front, which side the
loons favoured, a stump cut with a saw where saws aren't allowed, a fresh blaze, how deep the water is at a landing
compared to last year. He reports things and rarely explains them. The player learns to read his notes the way he read
the country.

**Dry humour, mostly at his own expense or the weather's.** He never jokes about the brother. He jokes about his
knees, the canoe, the coffee, other people's gear and the Forest Service, and always in half a line.

**Private.** He never addresses a reader and never explains who someone is. People appear by initials or nicknames
he never decodes.

**Feeling comes through things.** He doesn't say "I miss him". He writes "Two cups out of habit. Poured one in the
lake." Grief, fear and guilt all show up as an action or an object, once, and then the entry moves on to the fish.

**Never plain about the secret.** This is the main constraint, in the next section.

## Part 3. What Walt never writes plainly, and how he writes around it

The owner's canon: Walt never wrote plain directions, so that the notebook would keep the secret even if someone found
it. The player puts it together from his notebook and what people say.

| Never written plainly | How it appears instead |
|---|---|
| The cache, the documents | An ordinary-sounding word used as if it meant ordinary things. **Working term: "the cellar"** ("Looked in on the cellar. Dry."). The bible can replace it, but there must be exactly one term, used the same way every time. |
| Where the cache is | Never a lake name. A private nickname for the lake, a rod count, a bearing, a landmark ("the leaning cedar"), or a fishing note that only works as a route ("trolled from the burnt point toward the notch, 3 pulls"). Each clue page gives one piece, never two. |
| The secret portage | It has no rods on any map, so he never gives it a length. It appears as a non-event: "Took the long way round to the beaver flowage." He never writes portage there. |
| The cabin | "the camp", and nothing else. He mentions chores there as if it were a fish camp everyone knew about ("New sill on the camp. Old rail does fine."). Every campsite is a "site", never a camp, so the word stays free. |
| His brother | Never a full name. **V.** (from *veli*, Finnish for brother) or "him". Once or twice "my brother", only in the quiet years and only about boyhood. The bible owns the name, and the notebook never prints it. |
| The brother's death | Never "murdered" or "killed". At most: "They said he drowned. He swam better than me." Said once. |
| The company | Never its name, old or new. "the old outfit", "them", "the new sign". When it comes back: "Same men, new letterhead." |
| The men | Initials, or a description he'll recognise ("the one with the ring", "Mr. Clean Boots"). Never a name. |
| The boys | Nicknames only. Each of the boys gets one fixed nickname for the whole notebook. The informant's nickname must appear in warm entries in the quiet years and in one or two lines in the watched years that read differently on a second pass. |
| The escape plan | Chores and stashes written as camping habits: "Left a can of white gas under the rock at the narrows. Old guide's habit." Each one has to read as normal when you first see it. |
| Canada | Never "Canada" as a plan. Border words that a canoeist would use anyway: "north of the line", "the other side". |

**Rule of one meaning.** Every code word stands for one thing for the whole notebook. If "the cellar" means the cache,
he never uses it for a real root cellar.

**Rule of innocent reading.** Every clue line has to pass for a fishing, weather or campsite note to someone who
doesn't know the story. If it only makes sense as a clue, rewrite it.

**Overwrites in a second pen.** Walt rereads. On some older pages, a second ink (pencil, or a different pen) adds a
bracketed line years later, like `[Wrong. It was the S. arm. —W]` or `[Last year of the old dock.]`. This is
how the watched years reach back into the quiet ones, and it lets clue pages be corrected without Walt writing a plain
sentence. Use it sparingly: about one page in eight.

## Part 4. Walt's shorthand key

Writers use these exactly as listed. The book's journal tab may later show a key for each term once a keeper explains
it. That is a moment of need, so it doesn't break the teach-once rule.

| Mark | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `r` | rods (his own pacing; can be a few rods off the map) | `P 142r, rocky middle` |
| `P` | portage | `P L of the falls, 30r` |
| `L` / `R` | landing on the left/right as you travel | `P R, 85r` |
| `1x` / `2x` | single / double portage | `2x today. Knee.` |
| `W10`, `NW 15–20` | wind direction, mph | `NW 15 g25, WB` |
| `g` | gusts | `S 10 g20` |
| `WB` | windbound | `WB. Read the can labels.` |
| `→` | wind veering/shifting | `SW→NW by 3` |
| `T` | air temp °F at wake | `T 34. Ice on the pot.` |
| `wt` | water temp by hand: cold / cool / swimming | `wt cold yet` |
| `lvl` | water level vs. his memory | `lvl low, 1 ft below the old high-water mark` |
| `*` to `***` | site rating (stars only; no 4) | `site ** — good pad, no view` |
| `x` | site, stated, not stayed (passed by) | `x NW site: occupied, 2 Kevlars` |
| `LT` `WE` `NP` `SMB` `PS` | lake trout, walleye, northern pike, smallmouth, perch | `2 WE 17–19, jig, the reef` |
| `rel` / `kept` | released / kept for supper | `NP 30 rel` |
| `COLO` `CAJA` `BAEA` `WTSP` `HETH` | bird codes (loon, Canada jay, bald eagle, white-throated sparrow, hermit thrush), an old guide's birding habit | `COLO pair + 1 chick` |
| `c.` | coffee (cups counted) | `c. x3` |
| `hung` | food pack hung | `hung, good limb` |
| `bf` | bear sign / bear visit | `bf on the P, scat, berries` |
| `pty` | a party seen | `pty of 4, Scouts, 2 Grummans` |
| `0 pty` | nobody seen all day | `0 pty 3rd day` |
| `V.` | his brother | — |
| `the boys` | his old trip partners (nicknames) | — |
| `the cellar` | the cache (working term) | — |
| `the camp` | his cabin | — |
| `?` alone on a line | something he saw and won't name | `Lights over the N bay. ?` |

Private lake nicknames: he has some for lakes he's known fifty years. The names are plain and come from something that
happened there (`Coffee Lake`, where he lost the pot; `Two Stump`; `Mummo's`, after his grandmother's lake). They aren't
witty. Each nickname matches exactly one real lake, and a keeper or one of the boys can give the match. The bible owns
the list.

Dates: `June 3` style. There's a header line at the start of each trip:
`June 3 — EP __ — solo, 9 days food, the old tent.` The year appears only on the inside covers, and he writes it as a
span.

## Part 5. Example lines in Walt's voice

These are register samples, not plot. Lake names are placeholders unless the bible adopts them.

**Weather and the day**
1. `Up 4:50. T 41. Fog sitting on the water like it pays rent.`
2. `SW 10 by nine, NW 20 by two. The radio was right for once. Don't tell them.`
3. `WB all day. Read the oatmeal box twice. Good box.`
4. `Rain on the fly at 3 a.m., the soft kind. Lay there and listened instead of sleeping. Worth it.`
5. `Ice on the pot. Coffee anyway. c. x3.`
6. `Calm till noon, then the lake stood up. Off at the second point and waited it out under a cedar with the jays.`

**Portages, sites, the canoe**
7. `P 186r. Mud from 40 to the top. They've put new steps in the steep part, which I resent and used.`
8. `2x. The knee votes no on 1x anymore.`
9. `Site **. Good pad, poor view, a fire grate somebody walled up the size of a hot tub. Took it back down to one.`
10. `x the island site, pty of 6, two dogs, a speaker. Went on.`
11. `The canoe has a new dent from the boulder garden. She's got so many it's hard to say which is new.`
12. `Landing gone to rock. lvl low, the old high-water mark on the cliff a foot over my head.`

**Fish, birds, animals**
13. `2 WE 18 and 19 off the reef, slow jig. Kept the small one. Thanked him. Ate him.`
14. `NP 34 rel. He looked at me like I owed him money.`
15. `COLO pair, no chick this year. Same pair as last, I think. Same rock.`
16. `CAJA took the bannock off the plate while I looked for the salt. Fair.`
17. `Wolves at 11, NE, three or four. Then not. Then the loons answered them, which I've never heard.`
18. `Moose cow in the creek mouth, water to her belly, didn't move for me. We both had places to be and neither went.`

**The old days, the boys, his brother (quiet years)**
19. `Passed the old drill pad. Birch thick as my wrist on it now. We cut that clearing in a week.`
20. `The boys would have had this site by now and the whiskey open. Too quiet. Good quiet.`
21. `Two cups out of habit. Poured the second one in the lake.`
22. `V. and I learned to swim off that rock. He was better at it. They said he drowned.`
23. `Sauna at home tonight if the knee gets me there. Löyly. Nothing else fixes it.`

**The checking years**
24. `Looked in on the cellar. Dry. Same as I left it.`
25. `Took the long way round to the beaver flowage. Dam's up a foot. Good. Keeps the traffic down.`
26. `New sign at the junction in town. Same men, new letterhead.`
27. `Left a can of white gas under the flat rock at the narrows. Old guide's habit.`
28. `Saw cut on a jack pine by the creek. Clean. Fresh. Nobody cuts with a saw up here without a reason.`

**The watched years**
29. `Green Kevlar again, 2 men, no rods. Third time since June. They wave like they've practised it.`
30. `Truck at the put-in with the engine running when I came out. Didn't pass me. Was waiting.`
31. `Told the boys I'd be on the south route. Went north. Will see who asks about the south.`
32. `Someone was through my pack at the bunkhouse. Folded it back better than I do. That's how I knew.`
33. `Slept in the canoe, off the point, tied to a snag. Old man's foolishness. Slept fine.`
34. `Don't write it. Don't write it. Fish 0. Wind W 10.`
35. `Lights over the N bay, low, steady, then gone. ?`

**Strange things (side-story register)**
36. `Something big walked the ridge behind camp at 2. Two legs by the sound. I've heard bears on two legs. Not like that. ?`
37. `Found an old tin of bottles in the rocks at the beaver pond. Prohibition, by the glass. Left it. Some other fool can have the glory.`
38. `Bit of aluminum in the blowdown, riveted, painted. Old plane. Said a word over it.`

**The last page** (shape only; the bible writes the real one)
39. `They're coming tonight. I heard the`
40. `Not the cellar. Do not go to the`
41. `Pack stays. Tent stays. If you are reading this`

## Part 7. Do / Don't

**Do**
- Open each day with conditions: the time, the temperature, the wind. Close it with the fish, the birds, or `0 pty`.
- Use real rod lengths. When an entry gives a rod count for a real portage, it has to match the map data within a few
  rods, because players will check it against their map sheets.
- Keep humour to half a line, and point it at himself, the weather, gear, the Forest Service or other campers' fire grates.
- Show feeling through an action or an object, once per entry at most, and then go back to the fish.
- Let the three stretches show in length and in what he counts, never in what he says about himself.
- Keep every code word to one meaning across the whole notebook (`the cellar`, `the camp`, `V.`, each boy's
  nickname).
- Make every clue page pass as an ordinary trip page on first reading.
- Leave gaps. Torn-out pages, a trip with only the header line, a page of nothing but wind readings.
- Let him be good at this. He's competent, calm, and right about weather.

**Don't**
- Don't use purple prose, or sunsets described for their own sake. He'd write `Sunset. Good one.` at most.
- Don't let him address a reader, explain who someone is, or recap the backstory. The only exception is the last page,
  where he may write *If you are reading this* and not finish it.
- Don't write a full name, the company's name, or the cache, the cabin or the secret portage in plain words.
- Don't use "ring" for anything but Brekke. A fire ring is a `grate`; a water line on rock is the `high-water mark`.
  Don't call a campsite a camp, or the cabin anything but `the camp`.
- Don't write plain directions. A clue page holds one piece only.
- Don't use real people's names, real outfitter names or real business names. Town is "town", and the outfitter is
  "the store" or "the bunkhouse", never "the shop". Real lakes and real public history can appear as texture only.
- Don't use Ojibwe sacred material, and don't let him interpret pictographs. If he passes a pictograph site he writes
  `Paintings. Kept going.`
- Don't overdo the Finnish. One word every dozen pages at most (*sisu*, *löyly*, *perkele* under his breath, *voi voi*,
  *veli* only inside `V.`). Don't use the "you betcha" Iron Range clichés in writing.
- Don't make teaching notes. Items never explain their use: he never writes "hang your food so bears don't get it."
  He writes `hung` or `didn't hang, lazy, lucky`.
- Don't let him explain a strange thing. A `?` is all he gives it.
- Don't let him write about the informant as a traitor before the last stretch, and even then only sideways.

## Part 8. Notes for the bible and the build

- **Reading as play.** The rod counts, landing sides, bearings and nicknames are designed so the player can line a
  page up with the real map sheets and the real portage data. A keeper penciling a ring on a sheet is the right way
  to confirm a match.
- **Length budget.** A journal page in the book holds about 120 words. The sleep cutscene shows about 6 to 10 short
  excerpts before the last page, each under 25 words (taken from the samples like lines 1, 4, 13, 15, 21).
- **Second-ink overwrites** need a different style in the book (pencil grey, a slightly different hand) so the player
  can tell them apart without being told.
- **The bible must fix:** the years of the notebook, the code words (if it replaces `the cellar` or `the camp`), the
  nicknames for the boys, the lake nickname list, and which pages are clues.
