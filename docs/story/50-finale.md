# The finale: the far end, the camp, the cellar, the run, the endings

The last stretch of the main story, from the white stone at the beaver dam to the postcard. It follows
`00-owner-decisions.md` and `01-bible.md` (sections 5.2 locks 4–6, 5.3.6, 6.5 3.4–3.6, 6.6, 7). Where they are
silent this file decides, and every new detail is listed at the end for the continuity check.

Read with `02a-voice-journal.md` (Walt's hand), `02b-voice-people.md` (everyone else) and `03-places.md` (x/z). All
lake, portage and campsite ids here are the v3 ids of `03-places.md`; the lasting keys are the names and the x/z points.
Look every one up again in v9 before it goes into code.

**How to read this file.** Plain paragraphs are design. Lines in quotation marks or in `code` blocks are finished game
text. Stage directions for designers are in [brackets]. In conversations, **Cond.** is what must be true for a line or
branch, **Gives** is what it hands the player (a lead, a ring, an item, a line on the Walt page), and **Tell** marks a
lie and how a careful player can catch it. The Walt page is always written in the player's own words, first person,
short.

House style: British spelling, as in the game's existing text. Wind speeds in Walt's lines and the design rules are in
mph (15 mph is about 6.7 m/s, just under the game's `FRESH` of 8 m/s).

## 2. The landing sign

### 2.1 What is there

The east end of Gull is a dead end in a dead end: a narrowing bay, burned to the rock on both sides in 2011, then
fifteen years of jack pine so thick it reads as one grey-green pelt. The bay ends in a creek mouth with a beaver dam
across it (pinned: the dam never moves year to year). Behind the dam the flowage steps up in a chain of ponds to the
south (data ponds 12195 → 4747 → 10620).

At the dam's back, on the east side, stands **a cut stump**, sawn flat about 40 cm above summer water, its top grey
and checked. On it sits **a white quartz cobble** the size of two fists. Beside the stump, driven into the mud at an
angle, is **an old drill rod**, a thumb-thick steel pipe with a threaded end, rusted brown, standing about 60 cm out of
the water: Walt's canoe rest. A canoe's bow fits between the rod and the stump as if they were made for it.

Nothing else. No portage sign, no worn landing, no fire ring, no flagging.

### 2.2 How it reads by season

| Season | The water | What a player sees |
|---|---|---|
| Ice-out – mid-June | High; the dam is overtopping in places | The stump stands in water to within a hand of its top. The stone is on it. The rod is under water. From the canoe it is a stump with a white rock on it, among a hundred stumps. |
| Mid-June – mid-September | Summer level; sedge and bur-reed thick at the mouth | The stump stands in sedge water. The stone shows only to someone looking from within about 30 m (the sedge hides it from farther). The rod's tip may just break the surface. |
| Mid-September – freeze-up | Fall low water (the seasonal-water system) | The water has dropped about 40 cm. The stump stands on a bared **step** of mud and roots, the drill rod stands clear, and a flat landing shows between them. Anyone looking at the dam sees a place a canoe has been put in many times. **This is the "best found in fall" detail: the fall landing reads without the stone.** |

[Builders: the step and the rod are props tied to the seasonal-water level; the stone sits on the stump top at every
level, never moved. The sedge is the aquatic system's ordinary sedge, densest July–August.]

### 2.3 The dot

- On the stone, from the canoe or on foot: **"White stone"**. No verb. Walt's stones are never picked up; a player who
  tries to take one gets nothing to press, which is the point.
- On the stump: **"Stump"**. No verb.
- On the drill rod (fall only, when it is clear): **"Old pipe"**. No verb. (Only someone Tauno has talked to about the
  crews' gear will think "drill rod"; the Walt page says so in the player's words, below.)
- On the landing between them, from the canoe: the ordinary **"Get out"** of any shore. Getting out here is like
  landing anywhere off a portage: the canoe noses in, the bow rests against the rod.

every player would pass. The bible's five keys (5.2, lock 4) are used like this:

### 2.5 The watchers here

From the first time the player is seen on Gull or Pietro (at tier 4 or later; bible 6.2), a man watches the two ways
into Gull: the Pietro end of P530 and the P256 narrows. Before that, nobody watches Gull, so the watching never points
the player at the lake. He never comes into the east bay by day and never lands. If the player has learned to read tracks (S4), the dot names **fresh
boot prints** at the P530 landings ("two men, boots, came and went"), and **none** at the dam: the company has never
stood here. The Walt page, once: "Nobody's been to the dam but him. And me."

## 3. The carry

### 3.1 Its shape

About 880 m, about 175 rods, never drawn on any map sheet, never ringed by a keeper (`hidden: true`). Walt never
gave it rods and never called it a portage. In the notebook it is "the long way round to the flowage" (N21), "kept to
the east bank" (N27).

It works like any carry: the walker, the trail tunnel and the portage verbs all apply. The verb **Carry** appears at
the dam landing as at any portage end, because the game treats it as one, but nothing in the world announces it and the
map shows no line. The book's map **pencils it as it is walked and inks it on arrival at the camp**, the only carry in
the game that inks itself from the player's own feet.

| Stretch | Length | What it is | What the player does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The dam's back | ~60 m | The top of the beaver dam: packed sticks and mud, a hand's width of water over it in spring, dry in fall. It feels like the wrong thing to walk on and is the right thing. | Walk it. In spring the walker sloshes; a loaded canoe overhead is slow here. |
| 2. The flowage | ~330 m along pond 4747 | A beaver pond 2–4 m higher than Gull, its shore a band of dead standing timber and sedge. The east bank has a faint tread: an old crew line, with **orange-dab blazes** grown over to smudges on the fire-killed trunks. | **Three ways:** set the canoe in and paddle or pole the pond (fastest; the canoe is then at the pond's head); wade the edge with the canoe on a line (slow, wet, cold after September); or carry along the east bank. Any works. Walt "kept to the east bank" when the flowage was high. |
| 3. The climb | ~490 m east and up 15 m to the knob | Doghair jack pine fifteen years old, 3–4 m tall, so close the canoe catches if carried. The tread is a gap more than a trail. The grey trunks the 2011 fire killed stand over it. Ahead, the one dark mass of green in all that grey: **the island of cedar** the fire missed. | Walk. A canoe carried overhead through stretch 3 is slow and loud (branches on the hull). A player who brought the canoe up the pond leaves it at the pond's head; nothing up here needs it. |

The orange dabs read as blazes only to someone Tauno has told (bible 5.2 lock 5, key B). Without that, the dot on a
blazed trunk says **"Burnt pine"**. With it: **"Old blaze"**, and the Walt page, once: "Orange on the blazes. Crew
line. Tauno said."

Players who did S8 (the sled, reading old clearings) see stretch 3 differently: the dot names **"Cut stump"** on two
knee-high stumps near the knob that were sawn before the fire, the first sign that somebody once lived up here.

### 3.2 Seasons on the carry

- **Spring:** the dam overtops; stretch 2 is a wade to the knees. Blackflies from late May.
- **Summer:** the flowage is full and still; the east bank is mud; mosquitoes in the timber (bugs.js: the treed,
  wind-sheltered curve at its worst). Raspberries in the burn openings in late July.
- **Fall:** the pond is down a foot, the east bank is firm, the tread shows. The jack pine is green among gold aspen
  sprouts and red blueberry. Walt's month.
- **Snow (late October on):** the carry holds the snow; the trail tunnel reads as a white line through the pine.

### 3.3 The return

The carry is walked at least twice (up, and down on the run), which is why it is "short enough to do twice". On the
way down at dusk, carrying the cans, the player comes out of the pine onto the dam's back and sees the bay mouth. That
is the start of the run (section 6).

## 4. The camp

### 4.1 First sight

Coming out of the pine at the top of the climb, the player is under cedar: a stand of thirty or forty old white cedar
on the knob's lee (north-east) side, the only living old trees for a kilometre. It is darker and quieter under them,
and the ground is needles, not ash. The camp is right there and does not look like a building until the player is
within 15 m: a low hump of moss and bark under the lowest boughs, the colour of everything around it.

Then the shape resolves: a one-room cabin about 2.4 × 3 m, low (a tall player stoops at the door), its walls of grey
jack-pine poles laid between pairs of rusted steel posts. **Untouched since October 2017** (bible 2.5, N42): moss on
the roof, needles drifted against the sill, a woodpile under the cedar gone silver, nothing broken in, nothing taken.
No animal has got further in than mice.

overview. Collision for a small interior; the door frames are low.]

### 4.6 The escape preparations

The camp was never Walt's way out; the door was Birch (bible 2.5). It was the place he could hole up for a day or two
on the run north if a blow came, and it is where he kept count of the plan. The player sees three things:

1. **The shelf inventory** (above): food, light, fire, dry socks, a tarp for one. A layover kit, not a home.
2. **The October marks** down the door frame: `'12 '13 '14 '15 '16 '17`. One a year, every October, from the first
   season of building to the last visit. There is no '18: "Didn't go on to the far end. Not with company on the lake."
   (N42). The Walt page: "He marked every October here. The last one is '17."
3. **The tally** across the frame: `50 · 63 · 121✓ · 214✓`. It is the run north from the back cover (P530 50 r, P27
   63 r, P24 121 r, P26 214 r). The ticks are the stashes already laid by October 2017: the dry bag at the top of the
   121 (N25, August 2017) and the Grumman at the far end of the 214 (N26, September 2017). **50** has no tick: the
   spare paddle at Pietro was laid in June 2018 (N28), after his last visit here. **63** never had a stash. The Walt
   page: "50, 63, 121, 214. The back cover again. He ticked two. The dry bag and the Grumman." (The second sentence only
   if the player has read N25 and N26; otherwise "He ticked two.")

That tally is the player's map for the run, in Walt's hand, found an hour before they need it.

## 5. The cellar

### 5.1 Finding the pit

Three keys point at it; any one opens the dot's verb (bible 5.2 lock 6, and the knowledge gate in 5.2).

- **The sketch** (the inset on Tauno's crew map, "E-7 (fly camp) after E.H. field sketch"; it is the only copy. The
  state's target list in Maggie's papers is a typed table with no sketch): a knob, a beaver pond drawn to its **east**,
  and a pit "~600 m" off on a ridge drawn to the **south-west**. Eino drew it mirror-wise. Standing on the true knob,
  where the pond really lies to the west, it fits only flipped, and flipped it puts the pit south-east along the ridge,
  about 126°.
- **Torn page 2**: from the knob, toward the midwinter sunrise (about 126° here). No player can watch a December
  sunrise in the game (the pit closes on 15 November), so the camp carries the line: Walt hung the back door square to
  it and pencilled a sunrise on the door's inside (4.2). In October the sun comes up well to the north of that (about
  105°); a player who watches an October sunrise from the knob has to go right of it.
- **Tauno's pit, and the core spill**: out of the back door, a faint line of grey-green **drill-core chips**, thumbnail
  size, in the moss and the ash, running up the ridge. They are 1976 spill from the brothers' sample bags. The dot
  names them **"Core chips"** only for a player Tauno has told about core spill; for anyone else they are gravel and the
  dot says nothing.

The walk is ten minutes of real time through the same young jack pine, along a ridge of bare gabbro ledge where the
fire burned the soil off. At the top of a gentle rise, in a little hollow where the soil held: **a square slump**, about
1.5 m on a side, sunk a hand's depth below the moss around it. In it, bunchberry grows in a square. In fall the
bunchberry leaves have turned a deep wine red, so in September and October the pit is **a red square in green moss**,
plain to anyone looking and meaningless to anyone who isn't.

### 5.3 Eino's note (finished text)

The paper is a page torn from a pocket field book, ruled in faint green, damp-stained but legible, the pencil gone

## 6. The run

### 6.1 What it is

From the moment the note is picked up until the player stands in an entry point's lot with the cans: a stealth
crossing on the game's own systems. **No campsites. No fire. Rest only in the canoe, tied off.** The watchers know the
player has the cans, and they want them before morning without anybody getting wet or loud. The player wants to get to
the meeting point without being held in a light.

It is designed for **one long night** (Gull to Lake One, about 10 game hours of travel, about 75 real minutes, plus
whatever hiding and resting the player does) and a **variant of two nights** with a day hidden between them (Gull →
Little Gabbro → Farm).

It is always reachable and always recoverable. Being caught sends the player back to the last checkpoint of the same
night, cans in hand (6.12). It never ends the story and never costs anything but time.

### 6.2 The rules, and where each is taught

All from existing systems. Each is taught once, at the moment it is needed, and most are taught by Walt's notebook or
the world rather than by words.

### 6.4 Being seen: the model

The player is never shown a meter. The world shows state: a watcher's light stops sweeping and holds; the radio
clicks twice; the loons go up; on the scanner, words. Designer numbers below are tunable.

**How far a watcher can pick the player out, at night:**

| The player's signal | Open water | Against a shore or among islands | In reeds | Under the turned Grumman |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| White headlamp on | 800 m | 250 m | 40 m | 0 |
| Red lens on | 80 m | 40 m | 10 m | 0 |
| Lamp off, paddling, moon up | 120 m (silhouette) | 35 m | 8 m | 0 |
| Lamp off, paddling, no moon or overcast | 50 m | 20 m | 5 m | 0 |
| Lamp off, still (drifting or tied off) | half the row above | half | 3 m | 0 |
| By day (any) | 1.5 km | 400 m | 30 m | 0 |

- **Fog or the dawn mist** (mist.js) halves every figure; a thick bank at dawn on Lake One cuts open water to 30 m.
- **Twilight** (dusk and first light) counts as "moon up".
- **The watchers' own lights** are cones about 150 m long, swept. A cone that touches the player within the figure
  above is a sighting; outside the cone, a watcher sees only lights.
- **The moon is real** (astro.js). A player who bought the permit for the dark of the moon has a darker, harder night
  to navigate and an easier one to hide in. Tauno says so once (6.15).

**How far a watcher hears:**

| Sound | Calm | Wind 8–15 mph | Over 15 mph |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paddling | 80 m | 30 m | covered |
| Bailing (Q) | 60 m | 25 m | covered |
| A pack dropped on rock, a canoe set down hard | 300 m | 120 m | 50 m |
| An aluminium hull knocking on rock (the Grumman) | 400 m | 150 m | 60 m |

**The states:**

| State | Trigger | What the watcher does | What the player perceives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unseen** | — | Posts, follows or sweeps by plan | Distant lights sweeping; loons calling normally |
| **Noticed** | A sighting under 2 s, or a sound | Swings the light to that bearing, drifts toward it slowly, sweeps there for about 10 game minutes | The light holds near you; two clicks; a scanner line (6.8) |
| **Seen** | A sighting held 2 s, or noticed twice within 10 game minutes | Paddles at the player's last position at speed; radios the lake and heading; Base moves the others to the portage ends ahead on that heading | The light comes on and stays; the scanner names your lake and heading |
| **Caught** | Within 25 m with a light held on the player for 3 s; or the player lands within 10 m of a posted watcher; or a watcher's light finds the player at a portage end within 15 m | — | The catch (6.12) |
| **Lost** | No sighting for 10 game minutes after Seen | Searches the last-seen spot for about one game hour, then goes back to plan, with the gates ahead still posted until the player is placed again | "Lost him." The lights drift back to sweeping |

**By day it works differently, on purpose.** The watchers will not close on anyone in open daylight: an October lake
may have a duck hunter or a late party on it, and Wade's men are told nobody gets loud. So by day a sighting never
becomes a chase on the water; it becomes **gates**: every portage end on the player's heading gets a man, and a landing
with a man on it is a catch. By night they can close in, and they can lose you. The player learns that **day is when
they know where you are and night is when you can disappear.** Walt's line N29 ("Back door to the Front Porch in one
dark") is the whole lesson.

**The country tells you.** Things already in the world warn the player about what they can't see, and the game never
labels them: loons going up in a tremolo when any canoe passes near them at night; a beaver's tail slap; ducks
flushing off a bay; and the watchers' cigarettes, a red ember at a landing and, downwind, the smell of smoke (the same
men left a coffee can of butts at the Isabella camp). A player who reads the country the way Walt did knows a gate is
posted before its light comes on.

### 6.5 Light, in detail

- **White** (H): the trail is clear, nothing is tripped over, pages can be read. Seen at 800 m on open
  water.
- **Red** (only with Walt's red lens from S3 fitted to the headlamp; then H gives red instead of white): enough to see
  the tread on a carry and the rocks at a landing, not enough to read pencil. Seen at 80 m.
- **Off**: on the water the player steers by the sky's line over the trees and the shore's shadow, and the canoe moves
  as fast as ever. **On a carry with the lamp off** the walker slows to about half, and on rock there is a chance per
  stretch that the player stumbles: the pack comes down on rock (the 300 m sound), or the canoe's bow knocks. With red
  light the chance is small; with white it is none.
- **The watchers' light**: a hard white beam. Being held in it is a catch at 25 m; at 100 m it is only being seen.

### 6.6 Wind and water

- Over **15 mph** the wind covers the paddle. Over **8 m/s** (the game's `FRESH`) open water raises whitecaps, and
  spray and the bilge come as they always do (Q bails, and bailing is a sound).
- **Swamping on the run resets to the last checkpoint**, as does an evacuation or hypothermia (bible 6.6). October
  water is cold: a player who swamps on Clearwater at 2 a.m. would be in real trouble; the game spares them the
  trouble and keeps the cost.
- The watchers stay in the lee over 20 mph, like anyone with sense. A blow is the player's friend on the small lakes
  and their enemy on the open ones: it keeps the watchers in, covers the paddle, and makes Clearwater and Bald Eagle
  dangerous to cross.
- **Lee shores** are cover twice over: calm water (no spray, no sound from the hull) and shore shadow.
- **Snow** (late October on): if the footprint layer draws prints in snow, prints at a landing tell One the player
  has been there, and he gains on them. A player who keeps to rock at landings leaves none.

### 6.12 Caught, and the reset

**Caught** (bible 6.6): a light in the face. Then Wade's voice: from the landing, standing on shore in clean boots, if
it is a road landing (Lake One, Little Gabbro, Farm); otherwise **over a radio** the watcher holds up on his thwart,
the speaker turned toward the player. One line. Fade. **Nothing is shown**: no hands, no grab, no talk.

Wade's lines (the first catch of a save always plays the first; after that, one of the rest, never the same twice in a
row):

1. "Evening. Long way from a site, aren't you."
2. "No light on? That's how people get hurt out here."
3. "That's a lot of weight for a canoe that size."
4. "Cold one tonight. You'll want to get home."
5. "Take your time. I'm not going anywhere."
6. (at first light) "Morning. Long way from a site, aren't you."

**The reset.** Fade to black on the voice, one held beat of the night's own sound (water on rock, a loon far off), and
fade in at **the last checkpoint of this night**: the pit (as it was at the lift), the last stash, the last portage end
reached, or the last tie-off, whichever came last. Cans in hand. Same night, the clock at the checkpoint's time. The
watchers are **reshuffled**: each leg has two or three posting patterns (which side of a landing the gate sits, how far
One trails, whether Three comes round early), and the reset rolls a new one, so the night stays learnable but isn't
memorised.

**What a catch costs:** time, and at most about 15 real minutes of replay. Nothing in the world remembers it: no line
from anyone later, no money, no item, no tier.

**The third try.** After two catches on the same leg, the next attempt brings one help from the country, chosen for the
leg: the wind gets up past 15 mph; or the dawn fog comes early; or the gate sits one landing back. It is never
announced.

**Saving and quitting.** The run saves at every checkpoint. Quitting mid-run and coming back resumes at the last one.

### 6.13 Leaving the designed routes

The run works anywhere. **If no meeting is set** when the cellar comes up, the goal is **any entry point an outfitter
serves**, and the watchers converge on the nearest few; at dawn the keeper's phone there brings Maggie (if met) or rings
the Ledger (if not) (bible 6.6). **An entry point no outfitter serves** has an empty lot and no phone; stepping into it
gives the thought "Nobody here. Not with these." and the player goes on. **South up the Isabella** (to Isabella EP 35,

come out somewhere.
